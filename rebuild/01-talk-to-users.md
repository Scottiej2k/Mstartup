# POST-MORTEM

**Status:** Draft
**Author:** N. Calloway
**Reviewers:** none yet
**Distribution:** you

**Summary.** In thirty-eight months I started two things, a company and a marriage, and I ran them both from the same playbook. You are getting this document because of what happened to one of them. I'm not going to say which until the timeline gets there. If I told you now, you'd read everything before it as a warning, and none of it felt like a warning. It felt like things going well.

**Method.** Timeline first, root cause last. Wherever I still have what I wrote on the day, I've pasted it in and left it alone. Those are marked NOTE. I believed every one of them when I wrote it. A few I still do.

**Blame.** In my industry a post-mortem is supposed to be blameless. You write down what happened and what made it hard. You never write who. This one says who.

**Start of timeline.** The rule is to begin at the first signal anybody could have caught. That's a Thursday in late July, in a hotel ballroom in San Francisco, at 8:26 p.m.

---

# 1
## Talk to Users

`T–935 days · Cash $31,870 · Burn $7,900/mo · Runway 4 months · Customers 0 · Co-founder: unsigned`

At 6:10 I'm in the hallway with a shirt in each hand, and Kyle is at the counter eating cereal out of a mixing bowl. It's his dinner.

"Where are you going?"

"The Founders & Funders Mixer."

"Oh no." He puts the spoon down. "Nate. Do you know what that is? It's speed dating. It's speed dating where only one side has a checkbook. The other side is two hundred guys in quarter-zips trying to be worth five minutes. You say *so what are you working on,* they say *oh, nice,* and it's the same *oh, nice* you get on a first date when you say you're in consulting."

"Some of the investors are nice."

"They're all nice. That's how you know." He picks the spoon back up. "They'll tell you they'll give you a call. That's what they say instead of *no.*"

I hold up the shirts. He points the spoon at the blue one. The blue one is what I pitch in. Kyle says it speaks for itself, and what it says is *I have read about this.*

"What do you actually need out of tonight?"

I think about lying. It never works with Kyle. He can't hide anything himself, so he assumes I can't either, and he's usually right.

"Something real to show Priya. By the fifteenth."

Here is the math behind that sentence. My last company was an inventory tool for small retailers. I spent eight months and most of my savings building it without asking one retailer whether they wanted it, and it died in March, in plain view, the way things die when nobody ordered them. Priya wrote every line of it that worked. She stayed anyway, on half salary, which I pay out of the $31,870, in a region where half a salary is a hobby for the rich. Two weeks ago I saw a subject line over her shoulder. *Your offer from,* and then the name of a payments company with a cafeteria. Offers like that expire. Hers expires on August 15. She hasn't mentioned it. I've been not mentioning it back.

Twenty-two days.

"Has she signed anything?" says Kyle.

"No."

"Has she said *co-founder* out loud?"

"She said she'd decide once she saw whether this was a thing."

"That's not a co-founder. That's a roommate. I'm a roommate. I know the signs." He goes back to the bowl. "My advice. Play it cool. Don't look like you need it. The first guy who looks desperate loses the room. Keep your options open."

"Does that work?"

"For other people," says Kyle.

I put on the blue shirt. In the pocket of my jeans is a folded sheet of paper that says *1. Two investors. 2. One follow-up meeting. 3. Don't explain.*

I own a plan. I always own a plan. Keep an eye on that. It turns out to be most of the root cause.

"Two outcomes," says Kyle, as I get to the door. "You come home with a card or you come home with a story."

"Which is better?"

"The story. The card is a number that never calls back."

---

The mixer is in a SoMa hotel ballroom designed by someone who has heard of ballrooms. The first thing anyone does is read everyone else's lanyard. They're color-coded: green for *raising,* blue for *investing,* orange for *seeking a co-founder.* Orange is the color of a man at a bus stop for a bus that's been cancelled. I am an orange wearing a green.

I'm also carrying an empty cup. I don't drink at these things. It isn't virtue. If there's a cup in one hand, nobody expects me to know what to do with the other.

By ten past eight I've had the same conversation three times. *So what are you working on?* isn't a question here. It's a handshake with a subtitle. I get a man raising a seed round for a compliance startup that helps other compliance startups. A woman who is "pre-idea, post-pitch." A guy doing Stripe for pets, who asks whether I know anyone at Stripe.

"What are *you* working on?" he says.

"Healthcare. Something."

"Love it," he says, and leaves to find a person with an answer.

That is the entire company. A Notion page titled *Healthcare, something?* and twenty-two days.

Somebody claps me on the shoulder hard enough to rattle the cup.

"Nate Calloway. Inventory guy!"

Cole Whitaker raised eighteen million dollars at twenty-six. He's thirty-one now, and tall, and so warm that you check your wallet afterward, find it's still there, and feel guilty for checking. We were in the same accelerator batch the way two fish are in the same lake. His company, Northbeam, sends refill reminders by text message. Pharmacy chains buy them by the million.

"I heard about the shutdown," he says. "Respect. Most people never ship anything. What's next?"

"Healthcare. Something."

"Love that. Keep it loose. Optionality, man." He says it the way other people say *God bless.* "Don't lock into anything till you have to. I've had three acquisition offers since March and I haven't signed one. On purpose. Stay liquid."

"How's that going?"

"Amazing." He checks over my shoulder for someone better, and finds them. "I'm on at quarter past. Catch you after."

It's Kyle's advice in a better jacket.

My phone buzzes. Priya.

**Status?**

*Holding an empty cup. Surrounded by seed.*

**Talk to one real person. Not a fund. A person with a problem.**

*That's not what a mixer is for.*

**I know. That's why I said it.**

On the stage, under a banner that says **BUILD SOMETHING THAT MATTERS** in a font that doesn't, Cole takes the microphone. His talk is called "Conviction Is a Muscle." Two hundred people turn to face him. I catch myself nodding at the first slide.

One person in the room has not turned.

---

She has her coat on. The ballroom is heated to about the temperature of a small argument, and she is in a long navy coat, buttoned, with her hair in a low knot and a lanyard with no sticker on it at all. She is standing eighteen inches from the exit. The stage is behind her left shoulder. She's watching the crowd watch it, like someone watching weather from the correct side of the glass.

I walk toward the sliders, which are near her. I tell myself it's the sliders. I pick one up. It has been a slider since at least Tuesday. I put it back.

"You look like you're waiting for a bus."

She doesn't turn her head. "I'm waiting for eight forty-five."

"What happens at eight forty-five?"

"I'm allowed to leave. My mother said one hour."

"It's 8:26."

"It's been an *effective* hour."

Her lanyard has a name printed on it in the ballroom's default font. **MAYA RAMEN.**

"Ramen?"

"Raman." She says it evenly. She has said it before. "My mother bought the ticket. She filled in the form on her phone, in the store, while a customer asked her about his prescription. Nobody has ever spelled it right on a form."

"You didn't pick a sticker."

"There was no color for *here under protest.*"

"That's the orange one. They just think it means something else."

That gets the corner of her mouth. Not the whole mouth. She looks at me for the first time, and I have the sense of being run through a small, accurate filter.

"I'm Nate."

"Maya." She glances down. "Your cup's empty."

"I don't drink at these."

"You just carry the cup."

"That's right."

She waits. I put the cup down on the nearest table. She keeps looking at me.

"Better with the cup," she says.

"Better with the cup," I agree, and pick it up again.

"It's strange to see awkwardness and confidence so close together like that," says Maya.

Behind her, Cole says *conviction,* and people write it down.

"Wait," she says. She still hasn't looked at the stage. "He's about to say *discomfort.* He stops right before it. Three seconds."

On the stage Cole says, "And that means leaning into the—" and stops. He looks out over the room. One. Two. Three. "—discomfort of certainty."

Applause.

"You've seen this talk," I say.

"A version of it."

I file that under *goes to a lot of conferences.* It stays filed there for nine days.

---

"So why are you here? You're not raising anything."

"My mother thinks there's technology that could help the store. She told me I work too much and should get out more, and then in the same breath she told me to go and look at technology." She holds up an index card covered in blue ballpoint, in round careful handwriting. I read it upside down, which I do too often.

*1. Ask if they know what a refill is.*
*2. Ask what happens when it breaks.*

"Those are good questions."

"They're hers. I've been holding this for an hour. Nobody's given me the chance. They tell me what they do and then they tell me why I'm lucky."

"What's the store?"

"A pharmacy. My family's. In Fremont."

A family pharmacy. I've done zero customer interviews for *Healthcare, something?* and here is one standing by the sliders. Something in my chest that has been lying down since March gets up. I know exactly what it is. It's the thing that built the inventory tool.

"What's the worst problem at the pharmacy?"

She takes half a second to decide. Nobody else in this room would have spent it.

"Inventory."

"That's the answer you give vendors."

Now she turns to face me. "Excuse me?"

"I built an inventory tool for eight months. It died, because I never asked anyone, and when I finally did ask, they all said *inventory* too. I think it's what people say when they want you to stop asking." There goes item three. "Sorry. You don't have to tell me."

A man in a quarter-zip drifts toward her, sees her face, and drifts off again.

"The worst problem," Maya says, "is the ones we don't hear from."

"Meaning what?"

"There's a man named Mr. Peralta. He picked up his blood pressure pills on the fourth of every month for nine years. One month he didn't come in. Nobody called. Nobody knew why. Maybe he'd moved. Maybe the side effects scared him and he quit. Maybe he was in the ER." She takes a cocktail napkin off the tray beside her and squares it to the tray's edge. Then she squares it again. "We found out three months later, from his cousin, in the checkout line."

"What happened to him?"

"He fell. On the second. He was on his kitchen floor for two days before the landlord came about the rent." Her voice doesn't change. "He's alive. He's in a home in Hayward and he doesn't know the cousin's name. That's one of the good endings. Sometimes we find out from the obituary."

My stomach does something unfamiliar, and I let it.

"So the problem is follow-up."

"The problem is that nobody's job is to notice." She's looking at the napkin, not at me. "It's my mother. She keeps a list on the back of a receipt roll. Every night after we close, she calls the ones who didn't come in. Her own phone, her own time. Sixty people in a bad week. She stops when it gets too late to call a stranger. There isn't a system for this. There's just my mother."

And I see it. All of it at once, the way you see a word in a jumble: the problem, the user, the buyer, twenty thousand independent pharmacies, an old woman with a pencil doing by hand what software should do. It is the best thing anyone has said to me in four months. My pulse is going. I'm aware that the woman who said it is not having the same evening I am.

"That's a slide," I say.

It's out before I hear it.

"I mean, that's the kind of problem that would make a good—"

"That's not a slide, is it?"

There's a question mark on it, but she isn't asking. She says it the way you'd go back and check a stove you already know you turned off.

I look at the card in her hand. *What happens when it breaks.*

"No," I say. "I don't have a slide for that."

That's a lie. Log it: 8:39 p.m. I have the slide. I have the headline, the font size and the gray of the attribution line. What I mean is that I'm not going to show it to her, and at 8:39 that feels close enough to the truth to say out loud.

"Good," says Maya. For the first time she smiles properly, briefly, and puts the smile away like a receipt.

---

"Eight forty-one," she says.

"You could stay four more minutes."

"I could."

She doesn't leave. She doesn't move away from the door either.

"What do you do, when you're not being sent to ballrooms?"

"I'm a pharmacist. I don't dispense anymore. I work for a nonprofit that rewrites patient instructions so people can follow them. I interview people about what they didn't understand."

"Give me one."

She checks whether I mean it. "A man was sent home with a form that said *take with food.* He'd been taking it instead of food. Three days."

"That's—"

"It's a perfectly grammatical sentence. That's the problem. Nobody who wrote it ever had to follow it."

I laugh. She looks pleased, and then annoyed at being pleased, and I already want to see that again.

"And you?" she says. "Besides the cup."

"I have a Notion page called *Healthcare, something?*"

"With the question mark?"

"I don't remove a question mark until I have a customer."

She laughs, once, and seems surprised by it. Then she tells me this is the first networking event of her life and she has formed the view that they are "extraction, with cheese."

At 8:44 a man with a blue sticker appears at my elbow. He has learned my name from my lanyard in the last four seconds.

"Nate! There's someone from Bluebird Ventures you should really meet."

Bluebird writes first checks. Two hundred and fifty thousand dollars is thirty-one months of runway. It is Priya turning down a cafeteria.

"In a minute," I say.

"She's leaving at nine."

"In a minute."

He goes. I am as surprised as he is. Maya is watching me with her head a little to one side.

"Your sticker's green," she says. "You should go."

"I know what my sticker is."

On the stage Cole says thank you, and the room comes apart into applause. Maya looks at her phone. 8:45. She tears a strip from the bottom of the index card, along a fold, the way you tear paper when you were raised not to waste it. She writes on it and hands it to me.

"I have to come home with a name. Yours, now. My mother will ask whether anyone followed up, and it isn't going to be me. She keeps a list."

"Does everyone in your family keep a list?"

"Only the ones who are right." She looks at me a second longer. "I'm guessing you keep lists."

"Extremely well-organized ones."

"I'm sure," says Maya. Her hand is already on the door. "It was nice meeting you, Nate. Good luck with the question mark."

She's gone before the applause is. Cole is coming down the steps at the side of the stage, shaking hands without looking at them. He's looking over everyone's heads at the exit. I assume he's looking for someone better.

---

I don't go and find the woman from Bluebird. That's zero for two on investors and zero for one on not explaining. I walk out through the same door instead, into the thin cold of Third Street, and at the crosswalk I notice I'm still holding the cup. I put it in the trash can on the corner. My hand doesn't know what to do afterward. I let it hang there.

**Status?**

*I talked to a person.*

**Was she buying?**

*Definitely not.*

**Great. Follow up.**

On the train I take the strip of card out of my pocket. **Maya R.** and a phone number, in small exact handwriting, the way you'd fill in a form you intended to be held to.

Kyle is still at the counter at twenty to midnight. Different cereal, same bowl.

"Card or story?"

"A number."

"*Wow.*" He chews. "Investor?"

"Pharmacist."

"So a customer."

"I'm not sure yet."

The spoon stops. "You're not sure. About a lead." He studies me, then shrugs. "Okay. Don't overthink it."

I go to my room and overthink it for an hour and ten minutes, in Keynote.

The slide is white. In the middle, in forty-point type:

**"There isn't a system for this. There's just my mother."**

Under it, in gray: *pharmacist's daughter, Fremont.*

It's the best slide I have ever made. I know that the way I know my own phone number. At 12:50 I send it to Priya with no message.

She's awake. She's always awake.

**Whose mother?**

I don't answer that.

**Nate. This is the first real thing you've sent me since March.**

Then: **Sleep.**

I read those three texts and feel the twenty-two days loosen a notch, and for one clear second I understand what I've just bought and what I paid with. Then I do what I do with every lead. I open Notion, make a new page in the customer pipeline and type up my notes while they're fresh.

At 1:12 the phone lights again. It isn't Priya.

**Cole Whitaker:** Good seeing you tonight man. Saw you talking to Maya Raman. Small world!! Drinks Saturday?

I read it twice.

Her lanyard said RAMEN. He spelled it right.
