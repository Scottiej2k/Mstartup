# The Founder's Note: Voice Guide and Samples  `PROPOSED`

Draft for Scott to react to. The Founder's Note is the signature of the book: the
short, set-apart reflection at the end of every chapter where Nate names the overlap
between building a company and building a marriage. It's the one place he steps back
from the scene and speaks to the reader directly. It has to be *earned by the
scene, in Nate's voice, and never a lecture.*

Read alongside `nate-voice.md` (the narrator) and the decisions in `decisions.md`.

---

## 1. What the Note is
A short, visually set-apart passage (usually 120-250 words) in Nate's voice,
written from the same "now" as the rest of the book: the days after Maya tells
him she's pregnant. It does three things:

1. **Names the startup principle** the chapter dramatized, in plain words.
2. **Shows the relationship twin,** sometimes stated, sometimes left for the
   reader.
3. **Turns.** It ends on an admission, a cost, a correction, or an unanswered
   question, never on a bow or a moral.

The chapter body does not explain the parallel. The Note does. If a reader skipped
every Note, the story should still work. If they read only the Notes, they should
get a wry, warm, surprisingly honest essay about starting things and staying.

## 2. Rules

### What a Note does
1. **Starts from a concrete image in the chapter.** A cup of water, a phone
   buzzing, a fax machine, a tiffin box. Never from an abstraction.
2. **One principle per Note.** Not three. If a chapter has two, pick the one that
   hurts.
3. **Costs Nate something.** An admission, an embarrassing detail, or a
   correction. He's teaching by confessing, not by pronouncing.
4. **Is plain about the principle.** He can say "ask the person who isn't buying"
   in ordinary words. The cleverness goes into the turn, not the principle.
5. **Trusts the reader with the twin.** In roughly half the Notes he states the
   relationship parallel; in the other half he stops just short, or says "I'll
   spare you."
6. **Sounds like Nate.** Dry, specific, self-deprecating, with numbers and
   the occasional aside. Jokes thin out as the book goes on, but a Note is never
   humorless.
7. **Ends on a turn, not a bow.** An image, a question, a small joke that stings, or
   a line that stops a beat early.
8. **Varies form.** See section 4. No more than two consecutive Notes in the same
   shape.

### What a Note never does
- Sounds like a business book or LinkedIn ("Resilience is the key to..."). See the
  anti-sample.
- Uses cliches: "communication is key," "relationships take work," "trust is
  everything." He finds the odd, specific version or says nothing.
- Uses second-person commands more than very rarely ("You should..."). He says "I."
- Explains its own joke or its own metaphor.
- Summarizes the chapter.
- Ends with "And that's the lesson."
- Runs past about 300 words. (Short ones, 40-100, are welcome; see Ch 17 and 19.)
- Uses bullet points as a takeaway box. (One structured Note, the "board update,"
  is allowed once as a flourish. See section 4.)

### Test for every Note
1. Does it begin with something the reader saw in the chapter?
2. Is there exactly one principle?
3. Does Nate pay a price in it?
4. Would it survive if the reader skipped the chapter?
5. Does it end on a turn rather than a moral?
6. Could it appear on LinkedIn unchanged? (If yes, rewrite.)

## 3. Format on the page
A horizontal rule, a small label, an optional principle line, then the body.

```
---
FOUNDER'S NOTE
Ask what's actually wrong.

[body, 120-250 words]
---
```

- **The principle line is optional.** Early on it works like a slide title. Later Notes
  drop it, and by Part 5 one Note says outright that Nate has no principle.
- Set apart typographically (indent, italics, or a different typeface) at book
  design time. In the manuscript, the label and rules are enough.
- Never numbered; never titled with the chapter number.

## 4. Forms (a menu, so no two in a row feel the same)

| Form | What it is | Where it works |
|---|---|---|
| **The Slide** | Principle as a title line, then the body flips it toward the relationship | Part 1-2, most common early |
| **The Confession** | Nate admits what he actually did | Any, especially Part 2-4 |
| **The Correction** | Nate revises something he told the reader in an earlier Note | Part 4+, used sparingly |
| **The Short One** | 40-100 words, when the moment is heavy | Part 5 |
| **The Memo / Board Update** | A brief structured update (one line of KPIs, then the human line) | Once, Part 4 |
| **The Question** | Ends on an open question to the reader, or to himself | Any |
| **The Refusal** | "I don't have a principle for this chapter." | Once, Ch 19 |
| **The Withheld Note** | Something Ray or Maya said that Nate reports without comment | Rare |

## 5. How the Notes evolve

| Part | Notes sound like |
|---|---|
| 1 The Spark | Confident, quick, funny. Slide-style principle lines. He thinks he's teaching. |
| 2 Discovery | More questions than answers. Starts to concede that the twin cuts against him. |
| 3 Commitment | Warmer, more exposed. Fewer jokes at the top. |
| 4 Growth | Self-indicting. Corrections. The board update. |
| 5 The Crisis | Spare and short. No cleverness. One Note refuses to have a principle. |
| 6 Maturity | Quiet, slower. He says what he's learned without the lesson-voice. Last Note (Ch 24) is reserved. |

The trajectory: Nate goes from *having a framework for love* to *admitting the
framework was never the point.* The Notes are where the reader watches that
happen.

## 7. Sample Notes

### Ch 1, "Ask what's actually wrong" (The Slide, confident)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Ask what's actually wrong.*

Every founder gets the same advice, usually from someone with a podcast: fall in
love with the problem, not the solution. I'd heard it a hundred times. I'd even
said it once, out loud, to a room of people who wrote it down.

What I hadn't done, until a woman by the exit gave me the safe answer and I
asked, "Is that the one you give everyone?", was ask a question and be willing
to hear the real one. That's the whole trick. Not the asking. The willingness.
Most of us ask the way we hold a cup of water at a party: for something to do
with our hands.

Maya told me about the people the pharmacy never hears from. I didn't know I'd
been handed a company. I only knew that for four minutes nobody in that ballroom
was performing, and I wanted to stay in it.

It took me a while to notice that the same rule applies to people. You don't
fall for the idea of someone. You fall for the specific, inconvenient thing they
tell you when you've stopped pitching.
---

### Ch 4, "The best feedback comes from someone who isn't buying" (The Slide, playful)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*The best feedback comes from someone who isn't buying.*

Buyers are generous. They say "looks good," because they want the meeting to be
over, or because they like you, or because saying no takes a kind of energy they'd
prefer to spend on lunch. I had a spreadsheet full of buyers who liked me. It was the most
dangerous document I owned.

The person who isn't buying has no reason to be nice. She'll tell you the label is
unreadable. She'll ask what happens after the demo. If you can stand it, she is
the only real product manager you'll ever have.

I've thought since about how carefully I'd arranged my life so that the people
around me said "looks good." Kyle was the exception. He said it with a mixing bowl
of cereal.

The relationship version isn't hard to guess. I'll spare you.

Ray poured me a second coffee before I'd finished the first. I took it as a
compliment to the coffee.
---

### Ch 5, "Interviews to learn" (The Confession, wry)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*There are two kinds of questions.*

There are the ones you ask to learn something, and the ones you ask so you can
hear yes. Customer interviews are ruined by the second kind, and so, I'm told,
are first dates.

"Wouldn't it be great if you had a tool that...?" isn't a question. It's a
proposal in a trench coat. I used to ask it a dozen times a day. People are kind
and they said yes, and I wrote it down as data.

I did it to Maya, too, at a taqueria, in the form of a funnel. She did not say yes.
She said, quite clearly, that she was not in it. It was the first useful piece of
feedback I'd received in a month, and I nearly missed it because it arrived as a
joke.

I'd like to say I changed that afternoon. I changed about forty percent. I
still use the funnel when I'm nervous. Ask Priya.
---

### Ch 6, "Ship the ugly version" (The Confession, warm)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Ship the ugly version.*

My first company died in eight months without ever shipping anything. I'd like to
call that a strategic error. It was fear dressed as quality. Every week I found
a reason it wasn't ready, and every reason was true, and the truest one was that
ready is the moment somebody else gets to say no.

The Loopback prototype was hideous. It had a button that said SUBMIT in default
blue. It crashed twice during the demo for Dr. Okafor, who looked at me
over her glasses and said, "So it works when you're standing next to it."

She used it that afternoon anyway.

I arrived at Maya's door that same week without a plan. I had a bottle of wine I
hadn't researched and an apology I hadn't rehearsed, and I said it badly. I
think that was the first time she believed me.

Perfect is a way of staying home.
---

### Ch 8, "Optionality is a tax" (Slide + Withheld, sincere)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Optionality is a tax.*

Optionality feels like freedom. It's a fee you pay every day in attention: the
half-second where part of you is checking the exit. Founders who say they're
"exploring options" are mostly exploring their way out.

The week Priya and I stopped hedging on the market (pharmacies, only
pharmacies), the company got easier to run, and I mean easier, not smaller. We
stopped spending a third of every meeting on what if.

Maya said it to me on a cold trail at six in the morning: *say it like you don't
have a backup.* I'd like to report that I said it without a joke, and I did. It's
the only sentence I've ever had to rehearse and then forget the rehearsal of.

Ray told me that week, "You don't get certainty. You get enough. Then you get
brave." Then he told me not to write it down, because writing it down would
feel like doing it.

I wrote it down. I've been trying to do it ever since.
---

### Ch 10, "Take money from people who'll be kind the week after you're wrong" (The Slide, wry)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Take money from people who'll be kind the week after you're wrong.*

Every investor is kind when the arrow points up. It's costless. The only interview
that tells you anything is the one where you ask them about the worst thing a
founder ever did to them, and watch what their face does.

Margo told me hers over bad tea. A founder had hidden a lost customer for two
quarters. "I wasn't angry about the customer," she said. "I was angry that he
thought I couldn't handle it." She funded him again the next year. She said it
took a while.

I've thought about that a lot in the context of who I let close. There are people
who are wonderful when I win. There are a much smaller number who would
still pick up the phone on the worst day and say the truest and least useful
thing: *I'm here.*

It's a short list. It's the only one I keep a real copy of.
---

### Ch 12, "Runway" (The Slide with numbers, sober)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Know how long you can last.*

We had fourteen months of cash. I could tell you the number to the day. I checked it
every Monday, the way you check whether a stove is off.

I couldn't have told you how many months of patience Maya had. Or mine. Or my
parents', or Ray's, or Kyle's. You'd think a person who tracks one number so
carefully would think to wonder about the others.

Emotional runway isn't a metaphor I love, and Maya has asked me to stop using
it. But it's accurate. Everybody starts with some. It's replenished by ordinary
things, a walk, a meal that isn't at your desk, a night nobody needs anything from
you, and drained by the things founders do best: being nearly there.

Cash runs out on a date. The other kind runs out on a Tuesday, and nobody sends
an email.
---

### Ch 14, "You get what you measure" (The Board Update, sharp)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Board update.*

**Weekly active clinics:** 77, up 33%. **Net revenue retention:** 118%.
**Team:** 14. **Morale:** I checked, and it's fine.

**Sleep:** down. **Dinners attended in full:** I can't find the metric. I looked. I
can find dinners attended. I can find the ones where I answered Slack. I can't find
the ones where I listened to the end of a story.

This is the whole problem with a dashboard. It doesn't lie. It just doesn't
contain what it doesn't contain.

Nobody at the company asked me to build a tracker for Maya's Tuesday nights.
Nobody would have. But everything I measured got better that quarter, and
everything I didn't quietly got worse, and I called it a good quarter because the
arrow was green.
---

### Ch 16, "Build what was asked for" (The Correction, self-indicting)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*A correction.*

In Chapter 6 I told you to ship the ugly version. I'd like to amend that.

Ship the ugly version *of the product.* Not of the apology, not of the fix, and
not of the plan you've made for someone who didn't ask for a plan. That's the trap
inside the advice. Speed is a virtue when you're wrong about a feature. It's a
vice when you're wrong about a person.

I'd priced three staffing agencies for Maya's family in a weekend. I did it in a
spreadsheet. It was fast, thorough, and, she said later, "the most generous
thing anyone had ever done for me that I didn't want."

The customer had looked at their hands. Maya had lined up her shoes. I'd looked
at neither.

I still think the spreadsheet was good. I've learned that isn't the question.
---

### Ch 17, "Bad news doesn't age well" (The Short One, spare)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Bad news doesn't age well.*

Bad news has a half-life. I'd guess it's about seven minutes.

After that it isn't news. It's a secret.
---

### Ch 19, "I don't have one" (The Refusal, spare)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**

I don't have a principle for this chapter.

I looked. I've spent most of my adult life converting things that happened to me
into things I could put on a slide. I sat down to do it here, and there was
nothing to convert.

She said she wasn't a project, and I sat on a couch and didn't take out my phone.
That's all I have.
---

### Ch 21, "The blameless post-mortem" (The Memo, quiet)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Repair without blame.*

Engineers have a ritual called the blameless post-mortem. When something breaks,
you don't ask who broke it. You ask what in the system let it break, and what you'd
build differently. It sounds soft. It's the hardest room I've ever sat in,
because there's nowhere to hide and no one to hate.

We did one, Maya and I, at our kitchen table on a Sunday, with tea and no laptop.
It went badly at first. I kept trying to find the root cause and she kept saying,
"That's a very Nate way to put it."

What we found wasn't a person. It was a system: a habit of solving things
so I didn't have to feel them, and a habit of being fine so she didn't have to
ask.

We changed two things. I won't say which. The list isn't the point, and Maya
says I'd turn it into a checklist.
---

### Ch 22, "Due diligence" (The Question, quiet)
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*The questions you'd rather not answer honestly.*

A good investor asks the questions you're afraid you'll answer honestly. They're
rarely the hard ones. They're the simple ones, the ones you assumed you knew: *Who
would you call first if something went wrong? Who does the dishes? When did you last
tell her something bad before it got worse?*

I had a deck for years. I'd built it for rooms full of people who wanted to be
convinced. I had no deck for a kitchen in the Santa Cruz mountains and a man
turning a kettle off before it boiled.

I didn't pitch. I answered. It took about four minutes and it was the most
accurate thing I've ever said.

I'm still not sure what he saw in it. I've stopped needing to know.
---

## 8. Optional device: Maya in the margin
Since Nate is telling this from just after the reveal, and Maya has read the draft,
she could occasionally answer a Note in a single line, in the margin.

Example, after the Ch 5 Note:
> *[Maya, in the margin: "I said the funnel thing. You're welcome for the
> material."]*

Pros: humor, warmth, a second voice, and it proves she's a full partner in the
book. Cons: it changes the book's form (she becomes a co-author of sorts), and
overused it undercuts the Notes' seriousness.

If used: rarely (four to six times), never on a Refusal or a Short One, and one
late margin note that isn't funny.

## 9. Anti-sample: the Note we must never write
---
**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Communication is key.*

In business and in life, communication is the foundation of every lasting
relationship. Being transparent about challenges builds trust with your team and
your partner. Remember: how you show up for the people you love is the greatest
startup you'll ever build.
---

Why it's wrong: it's a greeting card. No image from the chapter, no admission, no
cost, no turn, no Nate. The cliches ("communication is key," "foundation," "trust")
are exactly what we've banned. The Note we want says half as many words and
contains a pharmacy, a cup of water, and a joke that hurts.
