# Maya: Voice Guide and Samples  `PROPOSED`

Draft for Scott to react to. Samples are written as Nate would narrate them
(first person, past tense, looking back), so you can hear Maya against both
Nate's voice and Ray's.

---

## 1. The voice in one paragraph
Maya is precise, dry and quick. She says less than she thinks and asks the
question under the question, often in a way that sounds casual and lands like a
scalpel. She is funny before she is warm, and warm before she is soft. She is
allergic to performance: no networking patter, no vendor cheer, no filler. She
listens by asking, and she knows exactly when you're giving her the safe answer.
When she is hurt she doesn't raise her voice; she gets **polite**, and the
politeness is the alarm.

## 2. Rules

### What Maya does
1. **Asks the question that's actually the question.** Short. Often a "why" or a
   "so what happens after that?"
2. **Tests, quietly.** She gives people a safe answer or a small lie and watches
   what they do with it. Nate's first win is that he asks a follow-up anyway.
3. **Is funny in plain sentences.** Deadpan, specific, observational. No
   catchphrases, no quips for their own sake.
4. **Gets specific about people.** She names the customer, the detail, the
   cough syrup. She never says "users" when she means Mr. Alvarez.
5. **Is right about people more often than Nate is,** and it costs him
   something to admit it. But she isn't always right: she misreads his silence
   as indifference when it is fear.
6. **Retreats politely when hurt.** "That's very thoughtful." "No, it's fine."
   "Thank you for telling me." Nate is slow to learn that each of these is a door
   closing.
7. **Names what she needs late, and plainly, once she decides to.** Her big speeches
   are short and unadorned. She doesn't accuse; she describes.

### What Maya never does
- Performs a "strong female character" attitude. She is not sarcastic as armor
  every second; her humor has warmth in it.
- Uses therapy vocabulary as a weapon ("boundaries," "gaslighting"). She says what
  she means in ordinary words.
- Exists only as Nate's foil. She has her own concerns (the store, her parents,
  Arjun, her work) and those show up in her speech, unprompted.
- Lectures about the "real" way to build things. Her observations are about
  people; Nate draws the business parallels himself.
- Is cruel. Even in her hardest scene, she is exact and tired, not cutting.

## 3. Tells: how Maya shows what she isn't saying
- **Politeness.** "That's very thoughtful." = *you missed it.* "No, it's fine." = it
  isn't.
- **Answering with a question.** Deflection, and often her way of caring.
- **The exit.** She literally positions herself near doors: at the mixer, at
  parties, at her parents'. Later, she's near the couch, not the door. Nate notices
  the change.
- **Straightening things.** Receipts, mugs, the edge of a table when she's
  processing.
- **Going still and looking at his hands,** not his face, when she's deciding
  whether to trust him.
- **Quiet humor at the worst moment,** when she's actually scared.

## 4. Phrase bank (sparingly)
- "Say the real one."
- "And then what happens?"
- "Is that the answer you give everyone?"
- "I'm not asking you to fix it."
- "That's very thoughtful." *(the door closing)*
- "Okay." *(neutral and dangerous)*
- "You can be right and still not be here."
- "I heard you. I'm just not there yet."
- "Those are people, not leads." *(only once, the pharmacy scene)*

## 5. Maya vs. Nate vs. Ray, at a glance
| | Maya | Nate | Ray |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentence length | Short to medium, exact | Longer when nervous, over-explains | Short, spare |
| Asks | The question underneath | Clarifying, practical ones | The question behind the question |
| Under stress | Gets polite | Goes quiet, gets productive | Goes to check the tomatoes |
| Humor | Deadpan, specific | Self-deprecating, defensive | Dry, at his own expense |
| Says "I love you" | Rarely, and means it | Never, until he does | Through Denise's rules |

## 6. Sample scenes

### Sample A: Chapter 1, the mixer
*Setting: a hotel ballroom in SoMa. Bad wine, stale sliders, lanyards. Nate is
holding a cup of water. Maya is standing near the exit.*

She was the only person in the room not looking at anyone else's lanyard.

"You look like you're waiting for a bus," I said.

"I'm waiting for eight forty-five." She checked her phone. "My mother said one hour.
It's an hour."

"Why are you here?"

"She thinks there's technology that could help the store." She said it flatly, the
way you'd read out a grocery list you disagreed with. "So. Here I am, looking at
technology."

"What's the store?"

"A pharmacy. Family. Fremont."

"And what's the worst problem at the pharmacy?"

I watched her decide. I didn't know it then; I only knew she took a half-second
that no one else at the event would have taken.

"Inventory," she said.

"That's the answer you give vendors."

She looked at me properly for the first time. "Excuse me?"

"I built an inventory tool for eight months. It died. It died because everyone
I talked to said 'inventory' too. I think it's what people say when they want you to
stop asking." I lifted the cup of water a little, as if that helped. "Sorry. It's
an occupational hazard. You don't have to tell me."

She was quiet long enough that a man in a quarter-zip drifted over, saw
her face, and drifted away.

"The worst problem," Maya said, "is the ones we don't hear from."

"Meaning?"

"Someone comes in every month for their blood pressure medication. Then one month
they don't. Nobody calls. Nobody knows why. Maybe they moved. Maybe they got
scared of the side effects and quit. Maybe they're in the ER." She straightened
the little paper napkin on the tray beside her, then straightened it again. "We
find out three months later when a cousin mentions it. Sometimes we find out
from an ambulance."

"So the problem is...the follow-up."

"The problem is nobody's job is to notice." She looked at me. "That's not a
slide, is it?"

"No," I said. "I think it might be the only slide."

She almost smiled. Then she checked her phone again and said, "Eight
forty-one."

"You could stay four more minutes."

"I could," said Maya. She didn't leave. She also didn't move away from the door.
I noticed that. It would take me a long time to understand it.

### Sample B: Chapter 2, dating-app culture
*Setting: a coffee shop in the Mission. Second date, sort of.*

"Can I ask you something?" she said. "Are you on the apps?"

"I was. I deleted them last month."

"Why?"

"Because I was spending forty minutes a night deciding who I wasn't going to
text back."

"Only forty?" She stirred her coffee, which had no sugar in it. "I had a
spreadsheet. Sixty-one matches. Fourteen conversations. Three dates. Zero
people who wanted anything more than someone to say 'so busy!' to."

"You had a spreadsheet."

"It was a coping mechanism."

"I have a Notion page."

"That," said Maya, "is a different, worse coping mechanism."

We both laughed. Then she said, "Everyone here is optimizing. Even the way they
meet people. It's like they're afraid that if they pick, they'll find out the
one they didn't pick was better."

"Are you?"

"Afraid?" She thought about it. She actually thought about it, which I was
learning was the rarest thing a person could do at a table. "Not of picking.
I'm afraid of picking someone who's still keeping the door open."

I said something clever. I'd love to remember what it was. It wasn't the right
thing. The right thing would have been to say nothing, and let the door she was
talking about be a door I could see myself closing.

### Sample C: Chapter 7, the pharmacy counter
*Setting: Raman Family Pharmacy, a Saturday. Nate has come to meet her parents.
Suresh has just gone into the back to fix a prescription. Nate is looking at the
old fax machine.*

"That," I said, "is a genuine wedge."

"A what?"

"Your dad still faxes prescriptions. Every clinic in this county still sends
them by fax. If we could—" I could hear myself speeding up. "Every pharmacy
like this is a lead. Hundreds of them. A single integration and—"

"Nate."

"Sorry. I just think there's a—"

"Nate." She hadn't raised her voice. She'd put down the bottle she was holding,
very carefully, as if it might spill. "Do you see that woman by the vitamins?"

I looked. An older woman in a cardigan, reading a label.

"That's Mrs. Chen. Her husband has Parkinson's. My mother knows what time he
takes each pill. She knows when he's had a bad week because Mrs. Chen buys
the good tea." Maya's eyes stayed on the counter. "Those are people. Not
leads."

"I know that."

"I know you know it." She straightened the receipt pad, squared it to the edge
of the counter. "I'm not asking you to not see a market. I'm asking you not to
see it here. Not today. This is where I grew up."

It was quiet. Somewhere in the back, her father was running a label printer.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. I heard you."

She nodded once. She looked at my hands, not my face. Then she said, "The fax
machine's been broken since March. My dad's been faxing from the UPS Store.
If you want to ask him about that, I think he'd like to be asked."

That was the whole speech. She didn't say *and don't do it again.* She left the
door open, six inches. It was up to me whether I walked through it or stood
outside explaining.

### Sample D: Chapter 8, deciding to commit
*Setting: 6 a.m., a trail. They are walking after a fight three days earlier.*

"I made a spreadsheet," I said.

"Of course you did."

"I threw it away. I mean I deleted it. I mean—" I stopped. "It had a column for
reasons to stay together and a column for reasons not to. And I realized the
second column was empty and the first column was a list of things you'd do
for me."

"That's not a great sign."

"It wasn't the reasons. It was the column. I was keeping score of whether you'd
be good for me." I could hear my breath in the cold. "I don't want to be with you
because it's efficient."

Maya kept walking. She didn't look at me.

"I've done the other thing," she said. "Picked someone because it made sense.
Because he seemed impressive. It was like... renting a very nice apartment I
never lived in."

"Maya."

"I don't want to be chosen because it's convenient. I don't want you to choose
me and then forget you did." She stopped. Her jaw did something small. "I want
someone who chooses me again on a Tuesday. On the days it's not fun."

I said, "I'll choose you on Tuesdays."

"That's not the sentence."

"What's the sentence?"

She turned around. The light was coming up behind her, gray and then gold. "Say
it like you don't have a backup."

"I don't have a backup."

"Okay." She let out a breath I hadn't known she'd been holding. "Me either."

### Sample E: Chapter 16, "I'm not asking you to fix it"
*Setting: the apartment they share, late. She has just come home from a 13-hour day
at the pharmacy, her parents' expansion now half-built. Nate has been waiting up,
with an idea.*

"So I did a little research," I said. "There are staffing agencies that place
pharmacy techs. Part-time, vetted, ninety days trial. I already priced it. I
could cover the first quarter."

Maya was taking off her shoes. She stopped with one in her hand.

"You priced it."

"I made a comparison. Three options. It's, uh." I pulled out my phone. "It's
in a doc."

"Okay."

"I just thought, you know, if they won't hire someone, we could hire someone for
you, and they wouldn't have to—"

"That's very thoughtful."

I should have heard it. I'd heard it before, three times, and each time I told
myself it meant *thank you.*

"You don't like it," I said.

"I like that you care." She set the shoe down beside the other one. She lined
them up. "Nate, I'm not asking you to fix it."

"But it *can* be fixed."

"I know it can be fixed." She sat on the arm of the couch, not on the couch.
"My father told me last week he didn't need to hire anyone because 'I have
a daughter.' And I stood there, and I said okay. And I drove home, and I
thought, *I'd like someone to ask me how that felt.*"

"How did it feel?"

"Like being a column in a spreadsheet." She said it almost kindly. "Thank
you. Really. That's a very generous plan."

She got up and put a kettle on. The politeness stayed in the room like a
smell.

### Sample F: Chapter 19, "Am I a project?"
*Setting: Nate's laptop, open on the counter. Maya has just seen a Notion page
titled "Life OS." One of the sections is "Relationships." Under it: "Maya:
weekly check-in, biweekly date night, monthly family dinner." Each has a
checkbox. Some are checked.*

I came out of the bathroom and she was standing at the counter with her coat still
on. She hadn't closed the laptop. She was just looking at it.

"Maya, that's a—"

"I know what it is."

"It's a planning tool. It's for me. It's not, it doesn't mean—"

"I know what it is, Nate." She said it gently, which was worse. "You've been
very good at this."

"At what?"

"At me." She finally turned around. She wasn't crying. She looked like someone
who'd been carrying something up a long flight of stairs and had just reached the
landing. "You show up. You ask the right questions. You never miss the dinners.
You remember my mother's birthday, and my father's blood pressure, and how I
take my coffee. I've been trying to find the thing you do wrong, and it isn't
there. It's just...I can hear the checkbox."

"That isn't fair."

"You're right. It isn't fair. It's true, but it isn't fair." She put her keys
in the bowl. It was the first time I'd seen her put them there. "I'm not angry.
I think I've been waiting to be angry, so I'd know what to do."

"What do you want me to do?"

"That's the thing. You want a task."

I stood there. She was right. I'd said it the way I'd say it to Priya.

"I don't want to be a project," Maya said. "I'm not a market. I'm not a launch.
You're good at those. I don't want to be good for you, and I don't want to be
handled. I want to be *here* with you and not be on a roadmap."

"I don't know how to do that," I said, and it was the first entirely true
thing I'd said in a month.

"I know." She took off her coat and hung it up. It was the first time in
weeks she'd hung it up. "Neither do I. But I'd rather not know it with you than
know it with someone who's good at it."

She sat down on the couch. Not on the arm. On the couch.

I sat down next to her. I didn't say anything. I didn't take out my phone. It was
the hardest thing I had done that year, including the seed round.

Somewhere in the pit of my stomach a girl named Hannah said, *I never felt like I
was in your plan.* I hadn't thought about that in years.

### Sample G: With Jules (the skeptical ally)
*Setting: a taqueria in the Mission. Jules is eating a burrito with terrifying
focus.*

"So he asked what the pharmacy's worst problem was," Jules said.

"Yes."

"And you told him."

"...Eventually."

"And he wrote it down."

"He didn't write it down. He just kept asking."

Jules chewed thoughtfully. "Are you being kind, or are you being useful?"

"To who?"

"Him. Yourself. Whichever." Jules wiped her hands on a napkin. "You did this
with Adrian. You made yourself such a good listener that he never had to be one."

"That's not fair."

"It's not *unfair.*" She reached across and stole a chip. "I just want you to
make him do the reps."

Maya looked at her plate. "He asked a follow-up."

"Everyone can ask one follow-up."

"He asked a second one."

Jules put down the burrito. "Oh, no," she said. "You like him."

## 7. Anti-sample: the Maya we must never write
> "Nate, I need you to know that I'm feeling really unheard right now, and I need
> you to hold space for me. Communication is the foundation of any relationship."

Why it's wrong: therapy vocabulary as a script, no specifics, no humor, states the
theme, and makes her a mouthpiece. Maya would say something plainer and more
exact: "I told you about my father on Tuesday, and on Thursday you sent me a
staffing agency."

3. **Maya's family in her voice:** should she code-switch with her parents (a few
