# Nate: Narrator Voice Guide and Samples  `PROPOSED`

Draft for Scott to react to. Nate is the book's narrator, so this is the voice the
reader lives in. Ray and Maya are heard *through* him; the guide is built to sit
alongside `ray-voice.md` and `maya-voice.md`.

---

## 1. The voice in one paragraph
Nate narrates like a smart, self-aware engineer telling you a story about the
worst-run project of his life: himself. He's wry, quick, precise about facts
and dodgy about feelings. He explains too much, jokes at his own expense before
you can, and reaches for a metaphor when he should reach for a person. He is
warm underneath, but he'd rather hand you a list than say so. Over the book
his prose changes: early on it's brisk and defensive, with lots of lists,
numbers and jokes. By the end it's quieter, slower, and full of what he has
learned to notice.

## 2. Rules

### What Nate's narration does
1. **Specific over generic.** The dead plant, the water cup, "eleven Slack
   messages during the cake." Never "I was stressed."
2. **Jokes at his own expense first.** He gets there before the reader does. The
   self-deprecation is a shield, and the reader should slowly see that.
3. **Uses lists and numbers.** He quantifies things (31 customer interviews, 11
   Slack messages). Lists are how he thinks and how he hides. The list device
   fades late in the book, and its absence is a signal.
4. **Reaches for engineering metaphors and lets them fail.** Funnels, sprints,
   runways, loops. Maya calls them out. By Part 6 he starts one and stops
   himself.
5. **Reports the body, not the diagnosis.** "My hands were shaking" rather than "I
   was nervous." When he does name a feeling, he names it badly, like a
   bug ticket, and the reader hears the gap.
6. **Over-explains when nervous.** In *dialogue* especially. His narration is
   more composed than his speech; the gap between them is a running tell.
7. **Trusts the scene.** Reflection is saved for the Founder's Note. In the
   chapter itself he stays in the moment.
8. **Retrospective, lightly.** He is telling this from shortly after the events,
   so hindsight lines ("I didn't know it then") are rare: at most one a
   chapter, and never a spoiler.
9. **Reads people better as the book goes on.** Early on he misses tells or
   misreads them ("Ray seemed distracted"); by Part 4 he catches them; by
   Part 6 he reads them before they speak. His prose sharpens accordingly.

### What Nate's narration never does
- Sounds like a LinkedIn post ("As a founder, I've learned that resilience is
  everything"). He's allergic to it, and mocks it when it appears in others.
- Explains the startup/relationship parallel in the chapter body. That job belongs
  to the Founder's Note.
- Uses more than one metaphor per page, or a metaphor in a moment of real pain.
- Is cruel about other people. Satire of the Valley is affectionate. Kyle, Cole
  and Margo are characters, not punchlines.
- Gets sentimental without an object to hang it on. Emotion arrives through
  things: a phone buzzing, a tiffin box, a kettle turned off early.
- Stays the same voice from Ch 1 to Ch 24.

## 3. Tells: how Nate's narration reveals him
- **The list.** He reaches for numbered items when he's avoiding a feeling.
- **The number.** "Thirty-one customers." "Eleven messages." He counts what he
  can't say.
- **The aside in parentheses.** Where he sneaks in the true thing.
- **The correction.** "I said I wasn't nervous. I'd counted the ceiling tiles."
- **The conversion.** He turns a person into a system ("top of funnel"), then hears
  himself.
- **The unfinished metaphor.** Late-book Nate stops in the middle of one. That's
  growth.
- **Short sentence at a heavy moment.** When it matters, the jokes stop.

## 4. Phrase and device bank (sparingly)
- "For the record," (before a fact he'd rather not admit)
- "I'd like to note that..." (defensive footnote)
- "In my defense:" (followed by no defense)
- "Here is a partial list of..." (a device that gets darker or sadder as the
  book goes on)
- "It took me another [N] years to..." (the retrospective sting, used rarely)
- "I did the thing where I..." (naming his habit)
- "Which is a very [sprint/funnel/roadmap] thing to say." (Maya's line, and
  his catching it)

## 5. Nate vs. Maya vs. Ray
| | Nate | Maya | Ray |
|---|---|---|---|
| Talks | Over-explains, jokes | Precise, dry | Spare |
| Narrates | Lists, numbers, asides | (seen through Nate) | (seen through Nate) |
| Under stress | Goes quiet, gets productive | Gets polite | Checks the tomatoes |
| Humor | Self-deprecating, defensive | Deadpan, warm underneath | Dry, at his own expense |
| Metaphors | Engineering, and they fail | Plain, concrete | Plain, from garden and machines |

## 6. How the voice evolves

| Part | Narration sounds like |
|---|---|
| 1 The Spark | Brisk, funny, defensive. Lists, numbers, asides. Misses tells. |
| 2 Discovery | Curious. More questions in the narration. Starts noticing hands and pauses. Still leans on the funnel. |
| 3 Commitment | Warmer, more exposed. Fewer jokes at the top of a paragraph. |
| 4 Growth | Frantic. Montages of metrics against missed moments. The list becomes indictment. |
| 5 The Crisis | Spare. Short sentences, physical fear, few jokes, few metaphors. |
| 6 Maturity | Quiet. Slower sentences. He notices before he speaks. Lists nearly gone. He stops metaphors mid-sentence. |

## 7. Sample passages

### Sample A: Chapter 1, opening the mixer
*Setting: a hotel ballroom in SoMa. Bad wine, stale sliders, lanyards.*

The first thing I noticed about Maya was that she was the only person in the
ballroom who wasn't checking anyone else's lanyard.

I want to be clear that this was unusual. The lanyards at the Founders &
Funders Mixer came with color-coded stickers: green for *raising*, blue for
*investing*, and orange for *seeking a co-founder*, which was the color of
someone waiting at a bus stop for a bus that has been cancelled. I had a green
sticker. I also had a Notion page titled "Healthcare, something?", no customers,
and a cup of water I was holding the way a hostage holds a newspaper.

(I don't drink at these things. It's not virtue. If I have a cup in one hand,
nobody expects me to know what to do with the other.)

I've told this story eleven times now. Maya tells it differently. In her version
I walked up to her at 8:03 with a plan. In mine, she was standing by the exit
like a person who had budgeted precisely one hour for this and was
already forty-one minutes into it, and I did not have a plan. I had a cup.

### Sample B: Chapter 2 or 3, Nate's morning (the list device)
*Setting: the Mountain View apartment. Early.*

My apartment in Mountain View had two roommates, one dishwasher, and a plant
that I want to say died in a tragic accident. It did not. It died the
way most things in my life did that year: slowly, in plain view, while I was
working on something else.

Here is how my mornings went:

1. Wake at 5:40. (Alarm at 5:30. I have never once obeyed the alarm.)
2. Cold brew. Not because I like it. Because it has a *system*: bottle, filter,
   twelve hours, done.
3. Open Life OS.

Life OS is a Notion page. It has five sections: Company, Health, Learning,
Money, and Relationships. Company was forty screens long. Health had a
checkbox for "sleep more" that I'd been checking on and off for years.
Relationships was empty. I'd told myself this was because it was "a Q3
problem."

4. Ignore Kyle.

Kyle was at the counter eating cereal out of a mixing bowl, with the
expression of a man who had opinions about my life and hadn't yet been asked.

"You look like a guy who's about to spend fourteen hours on something no one
asked for," he said.

"I look like a guy who's about to spend fourteen hours on something *someone*
will ask for."

"Who?"

"Investors."

"Not customers?" Kyle said, and pointed his spoon at me. "Just checking."

I did not dignify that. I made a list called "Reasons Kyle Is Wrong." It had
one item on it: "He works at a company that has a ping-pong table."

### Sample C: Chapter 4, early Nate misses the tell
*Setting: Ray's kitchen, Saturday morning. Ray has stopped mid-motion.*

I said "hypothetically," and Ray stopped.

It was about a second. His thumb was in the soil. He set the seedling down
and turned the little pot a quarter turn, and then he said, "Go ahead."

I remember thinking he seemed tired. I made a note of it, actually, the way
you'd note a slow server response. *Ray: distracted?* I decided to keep my question
short, out of consideration.

I've since learned that a man who is tired does not turn a flowerpot a quarter turn
before he speaks. A man who is tired sits down. Ray never sat down when I said that
word. He stayed on his feet, with his back a little straighter, the way you stand
for something you've decided to hear all of.

It took me about a year to notice, and about two to stop being embarrassed
about it.

### Sample D: Chapter 5, the funnel (the metaphor fails)
*Setting: a taqueria. Maya has asked why he asks so many questions.*

"It's like customer discovery," I said. "Dating, I mean. It's a lot of
interviews. You're trying to learn what the person actually needs, not what they
say they need. It's basically a funnel."

Maya put down her fork.

"Please don't."

"It has stages. Top of funnel is—"

"Nate."

"Awareness, consideration—"

"I am not in your *funnel.*"

"You're at the very top," I said, which was true in the funnel and
catastrophic everywhere else.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed, not the polite laugh,
the real one, the one I'd been fishing for since our first coffee. "That," Maya said,
"is the most engineer thing anyone has ever said to me on a date."

"In my defense—"

"You don't have one."

"I don't have one," I agreed. I abandoned the funnel in that same breath.

It took me another two years to fully abandon the funnel.

### Sample E: Chapter 14, "Numbers Up, Nights Late" (frantic Nate)
*Setting: the office, September.*

By September the dashboard had a green arrow on it. I want to be clear about
the arrow, because it matters to the rest of this chapter: it was beautiful. It
pointed up and to the right, the way arrows do in the decks of people who go on
podcasts. Weekly active clinics: 41, then 58, then 77. Net revenue retention:
118 percent. I screenshotted it and sent it to Priya at 11:52 p.m., and she sent
back a single fire emoji, and I felt something I would describe, if pressed,
as enormous.

Here is a partial list of other things that happened in September:

1. Maya's mother's birthday. I attended. I answered eleven Slack messages
   during the cake.
2. A Tuesday dinner Maya cooked. I said "that's great" about a story she hadn't
   finished.
3. Ray's tomato harvest weekend. I had a board prep. I told myself I'd go
   the next Saturday. It rained.
4. Kyle's birthday. I sent a GIF.
5. A Thursday when Maya sat across from me and asked, quite gently, whether I
   remembered what we'd talked about on our second date, and I said, "Sure," and
   it was so obviously untrue that she smiled at me with a kind of gorgeous mercy.

None of those had an arrow.

### Sample F: Chapter 17, "The Customer Who Left" (spare Nate)
*Setting: the office, late. Nate has just read an email.*

The email was four sentences long. I read it eleven times. I know because I
counted, which is the kind of thing I do instead of feeling something.

Meridian Community Health was leaving. They were our largest customer. They
were thirty-four percent of
revenue. The email said *thank you for your partnership.*
It said *transition.* It did not say *why*, and that, I understood, was the
kindest thing anyone did for me that week.

I sat at my desk. It was 9:12. Priya had already gone home. The office had the
smell that empty offices get, carpet and cold coffee and somebody's forgotten
lunch.

I looked at my hands. They were doing the thing where they didn't quite
seem to belong to me.

I wanted to call Maya. That surprised me. I did not want to fix it with her,
which was what I'd always done. I only wanted her to know.

I didn't call. I told myself it was late. I told myself she'd worry.

I've been asked, since, what the worst moment of the company was. It wasn't the
email. It was the seven minutes after the email, when I knew exactly what I would
do, and what I would do was open a spreadsheet.

### Sample G: Chapter 21, "Rebuild" (late Nate, and the unfinished metaphor)
*Setting: their kitchen, a Tuesday night. He is washing. Maya is drying.*

We do the dishes together now. It's not efficient. I've timed it. It takes
nine minutes longer when there are two of us, and both of us know it, and
neither of us has ever mentioned it, and I think that might be the whole point.

Maya was drying a glass. She held it up to the light, the way Ray does, though
she doesn't know that.

"You had a look," she said.

"I was thinking about the company."

"And?"

"It's like a—" I started. I could feel the sentence assembling itself: *loop*,
and then something about *closing*. It had a nice shape. It would have been the
best thing I said all week.

I stopped.

"You were going to say something."

"It wasn't good."

"Say it anyway."

"It was a metaphor."

"Oh, no." She put the glass down. She was smiling. "Was it about a funnel?"

"It was about a loop."

"You can keep the loop." She took the next plate from me. "I'd rather hear the
thing under it."

The water ran. Somewhere in the apartment, a neighbor's radio played a song I
almost knew.

"I'm scared it won't hold," I said. "The company. Us. I keep waiting to find the
part that breaks."

She didn't say anything wise. She didn't tell me it would be fine. She dried the
plate, and set it down, and put her hand over mine in the dishwater, and for a
while that was the entire conversation.

I'd like to note that it was the best one we ever had.

## 8. Anti-sample: the Nate we must never write
> "Looking back, I realize that building a startup taught me the importance of
> vulnerability. As a founder, I had to learn that authentic communication is the
> foundation of any lasting partnership."

Why it's wrong: it's a LinkedIn post. It states the parallel, uses no objects, no
jokes, no specifics, no gap between what he says and what he knows. Real Nate
would say maybe half of one sentence of it, and then make a joke, and then
list what he didn't do.

## 9. Questions for Scott
1. **Narrating position:** when is Nate telling this? (a) shortly after the
   pregnancy reveal, so hindsight is short and the ending stays fresh; (b) a few
   years later, with the baby already here (the reveal becomes a flashback); or (c) never
   specified, with just an occasional light "I've since learned." I lean (c).
2. **Hindsight lines:** Sample C and D each have one ("It took me a year..."). Is one
   per chapter the right ceiling, or should there be more or fewer?
3. **Lists and numbers:** the list device is a major part of his voice. Too much
   in Sample B and E, or right?
4. **Humor level in narration:** is the joke density in Samples A and B (roughly
   one joke every two or three sentences) right, or should it run lighter?
5. **Nate's profanity:** in narration or dialogue, does he ever curse? (Ray is mild
   and occasional.)
6. **Valley satire:** the lanyard stickers in Sample A. Is that the right level of
   affectionate mockery, or should it be sharper or gentler?
