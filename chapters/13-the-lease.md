# Chapter 13
## The Lease

Theo found the bug in an afternoon. It had taken me a week to lose it.

It was a Thursday in July, in the glass room, and the bug was a small one: nine patients in a row had been reported to their people as having gone quiet, although every one of them had answered. I had spent the week on it. I'd built a dashboard, and a second dashboard to look at the first one, and concluded, with the confidence of a man who has read a number of blog posts, that it was a *business* problem, having to do with carrier delays.

Theo had been with us for three weeks. The crack phone had gone home in Priya's bag in June, "just for the first month," and neither of us had brought it up since. He sat next to Priya in the hoodie, in the chair we'd bought with the second monitor, and he said nothing for about four hours, and then he turned around.

"It's the *a,*" said Theo.

"What's the a?"

"The reply. *Reply A if you're okay.* Nine of them typed a lowercase *a.* The phone does that. It capitalizes the first letter of a message, except when you're on a certain kind of keyboard." He held up his own phone, as if it were evidence. "We only counted the big one."

There was a silence. Priya, without turning her head, took a pen from behind her ear and made a small mark on a sheet of paper, and I understood that it was a mark in a column she'd been keeping, and that the column was labeled with my name.

"I've been on this for a week," I said.

"I know," said Theo gently. "Priya told me. She said don't say anything until you've asked." He scratched his beard, which had decided. "I wanted to see if you'd ask."

So I asked, which cost me nothing but the week. "How did you find it?"

"I stopped reading what we meant," said Theo, "and read what the phones sent. It's what I did at the Denny's. You don't ask the customer what they ordered. You look at the ticket." He said it kindly, and I kept it. It was a small thing, and it has stayed with me. I had a hundred and twelve patients, now that Margo's money had bought a second clinic, and a salary that was, for the first time, a number with a comma in it, and I'd spent five days building a wall around a lowercase letter because it was easier than saying *I'm stuck.* It turns out the comma changes what you're afraid of. I'd been poor for sixteen months and I'd known how. I hadn't known how to be someone with fourteen months of runway and a free Saturday.

That's how I came to be looking at a one-bedroom in Sunnyvale.

---

I told Kyle on a Sunday, at the counter, over the mixing bowl. I'd prepared nothing, which I'd begun to do on purpose, and which turns out to be the same as being unprepared.

"I'm looking at places," I said. "In Sunnyvale. It's the dot on the map that keeps Maya inside her forty minutes of the pharmacy and me inside mine. We might—it's a *might.*"

He stopped chewing. He held the spoon in the air for about a second, like a man in a cartoon who's walked off a cliff and hasn't noticed.

"Cool," said Kyle. Then, after a moment: "Does it have a balcony?"

"What?"

"Nothing." He looked at the bowl. "You've known her a year."

"A year and two weeks."

"Dude, it's a *lease.*" He put the spoon in the bowl. "It's twelve months, in writing, with a landlord. It's the same as a company, except the company can't cry in the kitchen." He said it lightly, and then not lightly. "You've never lived with anyone you were trying to impress, and I don't count, I'm a *mixing bowl.* You'll move in and find out she has a *thing,* and you'll be stuck in a one-bedroom with it."

"She doesn't have a thing."

"*Everybody's* got a thing. You've got one. It's that you've never been stuck." He pointed at my laptop. "And you've got a spreadsheet. Of course you've got a spreadsheet. Is she a row?"

I'd left it open. It said **Housing,** and under it, in a column I'd labeled **Variable,** in a cell with a small blue border, it said **Maya (50%): $1,700.**

"It's the rent," I said.

"It's *her.*"

"It's the rent *share.* It's just how I—"

"Look," said Kyle. He'd gone a bit pale, and his voice had gone up into a register I recognized from a dorm room in Ohio, at two a.m., when we were eighteen. "I'm just saying. You've got a company that could work or not. Don't lock yourself in. There's a thing called *optionality*—"

"You don't want advice," I said.

It came out level. It came out like something I'd been carrying, in a pocket, for a year.

"You want an audience. You've been giving me advice since we were eighteen, and you've never once asked how it turned out. You want me to need you to tell me what to do, because then you don't have to do anything." I heard my voice, and it was a voice I'd never used on him. "I don't need your opinion on my life, Kyle. I need a roommate. And you can't even be that without a speech."

He didn't say anything. The refrigerator hummed.

Kyle picked up the bowl. He looked at it. He put it down. He took his jacket from the back of the stool, where it had hung since March, and he said, in a quiet, even voice that I've never been able to forget, "Okay."

Then he left. The door didn't slam. It closed, gently.

---

It was eleven-fifteen when I called Maya, and I hadn't planned what I'd say, and what I said was the truest thing I had.

She picked up on the second ring. "You're never late. We're supposed to talk at eleven."

"I was late."

"What happened?"

I told her about the fight. I told her all of it, flat, like reading a report: the spreadsheet, the row, the thing I'd said. Somewhere in the telling I found I was sitting on the kitchen floor, with my back against the cabinet, and I heard myself say it, out of nowhere, in the same voice.

"What if we just moved in together."

There was a silence on the line. It wasn't the dangerous kind. It was the kind where somebody is turning something over, slowly, to look at the bottom.

"That's not a question," said Maya.

"No."

"That's a sentence you said because you're hurt."

"It's a sentence I've been saying to a spreadsheet for three weeks. I said it to the wrong person." I pressed my eyes with the heel of my hand. "I'm saying it to you now, on a kitchen floor, badly."

"Are you on the floor?"

"Yes."

"Okay." I heard her breathe. "I want to. I want you to hear that, before I say the other part." She stopped, and started again. "I'm not sure I'm ready. I've never lived with anyone, and I don't know what it'll do to me, and I don't know what I'll do to you. I'd like a day."

"Take it."

"Go to bed," said Maya. "Don't build anything."

I didn't sleep. I lay in a room that smelled of a roommate who wasn't there, and I listened for a door, and I took the spreadsheet, at four in the morning, and I closed it without saving.

---

Jules came to the apartment in Glen Park on Tuesday night, with noodles.

She'd been told. I could see it from the way Jules looked at me from behind the counter, with a box of pad thai, the way a public defender looks at a defendant whose file she's read on the train. Maya was on the couch in her gray sweater, knees up, chin on them. The chain hung on the door as if someone had meant to remove it.

"He brought a laptop," said Jules.

"I brought a laptop," I said. "I brought it to show her the place. I don't have to open it."

"Open it," said Maya.

It was a two-page comparison: three apartments, a map, a column of numbers for each, in a typeface I'd chosen on purpose. Maya read it in silence for two minutes. Jules read it over her shoulder, eating noodles, like someone reading the terms and conditions of a product she'd been sold.

"There's a row that says *Maya,*" said Maya.

"It's rent."

"It's rent. And it's a variable." She looked up at me, and her face was not unkind, and it was tired. "You gave me a row. I'm *moving in* with you. I'm not a line item."

"I know. I deleted it. Look, I—"

"It's not that you did it," said Maya. "You'd do it. I know you'd do it. It's that you did it *first,* in the place where you'd done it with everything, and it didn't occur to you that I'd see." She put the laptop on the coffee table, closed. "I'm not angry. I think I'm a little scared."

Jules put down her noodles.

"Can I ask a question?" said Jules. "Not a nice one. It's a public defender question." She looked at Maya. She looked at me, for about a second, and then back. "Are you afraid of him, or are you afraid of yourself?"

I didn't say anything. I'd been, all week, braced to be the subject. I hadn't thought of being a bystander.

Maya looked at her knees for a long time.

"Myself," she said. "I've been the one who doesn't need anything for twenty-eight years. I'm good at it. It's a whole apartment. There's a *chain.* If I live with someone, I'll have to be someone who's there in the morning, in a bad way, with my hair. I'll have to need the coffee to be made." She took a breath. "I'm afraid I'll find out that I do."

"That's not a bad thing to find out," said Jules, gently.

"It's a large thing."

"It's the only thing," said Jules, and picked her noodles back up.

It was Maya, at the end, who said it. She said it without looking at me, to the room, as if reading a clause.

"We'll decide together," she said. "Not you. Not me. The two of us, with a pen. We'll look at the apartment, and we'll both say yes, or we won't, and the lease has both names on it." She looked at me, then. "And I'm going to tell you when I'm scared. Not by being polite."

"Okay," I said.

"Don't say—"

"It's the one without the period," I said.

She stared at me. And then she laughed, suddenly, into her sleeve, and Jules, without looking up from the noodles, said, "I'd like the record to show that I've been asked to witness something."

We signed on the Thursday, in a leasing office that smelled of carpet glue, after seeing the unit twice: a second-floor one-bedroom with a window that looked at a tree. There was a pen on a chain. Maya checked it before she used it. She wrote her name first, and pressed hard, and looked at the line for a moment, the way you look over the edge of something.

"It's twelve months," she said, to the page.

"It has a landlord," I said. "It doesn't have a schedule."

"I'll take the landlord," said Maya, and handed me the pen.

---

Move day was the twenty-second of August, a Saturday, and it was a hundred and one degrees, and Kyle came with a hand truck.

I hadn't spoken to him in nearly two weeks. I'd texted twice, once something I deleted, once a photograph of the mixing bowl. He hadn't answered. I told myself that was fine. I knew how to wait.

At 8:05 in the morning there was a knock on the door, and there he was in the hall in a T-shirt that said something about a cereal, with a hand truck, and a box of doughnuts balanced on the handle.

"I don't know what you've got," said Kyle. "I figured there was furniture."

I didn't say anything.

"Also I'm sorry." He looked at the hand truck, carefully, as if it had instructions. "I've been writing this for thirteen days. I was going to do it in a speech, and then I remembered what you said about the speech." He set the doughnuts on the counter. "I said the optionality thing because I thought you'd stop needing me. That's the whole sentence. It took me a long time to get to it."

"Kyle—"

"I'm not done. I've got two more." He held up a finger. "One. I've been working on something. A site. It ranks cereals by how long they hold up in milk. It's called SogTime." He said it with what I can only call terror. "It has four users. One is my mother. She found a bug."

"That's—"

"Two." He put his hands in his pockets. "I'd like to give you a piece of advice. It's a small one. Do you want it? I'm asking first."

It's a strange thing to be asked that by a man who's been handing it out since you were eighteen. I looked at him, in the doorway, in the heat, with a hand truck, and I understood that he'd been practicing it in a mirror.

"Yes," I said.

"Take the armchair," said Kyle. "She'll say it's ugly. Don't let her. It's a good chair. It's held a lot of people up." He looked away, at the window, at nothing. "It's the only thing in this place we both picked, and I'd like it to go somewhere that has a person in it."

We carried it down together on the hand truck, in a hundred and one degrees, three flights and out to a rented van, Kyle on the bottom and me on the top, saying *left* and *your left.* On the curb he wiped his face with his T-shirt, and then did something I'd never seen him do: he stood next to me for a minute and didn't say anything.

"I kept the bowl," said Kyle. "You can come eat out of it on Sundays."

---

The Ramans had us to dinner the next night, the Sunday, in the cream stucco house in Fremont, and Lakshmi announced it over the rice.

"We are expanding," she said.

Nobody said anything. I had a fork in my hand. Across the table, Maya, who'd spent the day carrying boxes up a flight of stairs and whose hair was still coming out of its clip, set her glass down slowly, as if it might wake.

"The nail salon next door has closed," said Lakshmi. "The owner is retiring to Fresno. There is an empty suite, two rooms. A consultation room, for the pharmacist to sit with a patient, with a door. And a corner, for the vaccinations. So nobody has to go to the clinic." She said it all at once, as if she'd practiced in front of a pot.

"And staffing?" said Maya.

"We will not hire," said Suresh.

It was the first thing he'd said since he sat down. He was at the head of the table, in a white shirt with the top button done, with his plate squared in front of him. It was not said unkindly. It was said like a fact about the weather.

"Appa," said Maya, quietly. "It's double the space."

"Yes."

"It's double the hours. Arjun could—"

"Arjun is in Seattle," said Suresh. (Arjun was her older brother, an ER doctor, the one who'd gone away and succeeded. I had never met him.) "He is saving people." He said it with pride and no apology. "I have a daughter."

The room was absolutely silent. A clock ticked in the hall. I watched Maya receive it, the way you receive a parcel you've known for years was coming. She looked at her father. Her face went polite.

"Okay," said Maya.

It had a period on it. It had a whole sentence's worth of period.

I opened my mouth. I'd planned to say *I can help,* and *let's talk about a schedule.* I closed it. I sat there in the quiet of her parents' dining room and did nothing, which I'd learned on a guardrail. Maya reached under the table and found my hand. She held it, tightly, for about ten seconds, and then let go, and passed the rice.

"There's more dal," said Lakshmi. She didn't look at anyone.

---

I went up the mountain on the following Saturday, without a hypothetical, because I didn't have one. I had a feeling that has no shape, like a coat on a chair.

Ray didn't ask. He was in the kitchen at noon, in the flannel, frying two eggs in a pan that had been his wife's, and he said, without turning around, "Sit," and put a plate in front of me, and we ate. He washed the pan. I was halfway through the coffee when he held out a dish towel, at arm's length, like a man offering a cigarette.

"You wash," said Ray. "I'll dry."

"I can—"

"It's a rule. It isn't mine." (He didn't say whose. The pan did.) He ran the water. "You do the dishes together. Not because it's efficient. It isn't. It's the most inefficient thing in the house, two people at one sink." He handed me the first plate. "Because it's the only time nobody's trying to get anywhere."

I washed. He dried. We did it in silence, in the small kitchen, with the window over the sink looking out at the yard, at the string, at the kidney-shaped depression in the lawn that had acquired a sheet of blue plastic. A redwood dripped on the roof. I handed him a fork, a glass, a plate with a chip in the edge, and found that my shoulders, which had been up around my ears for a fortnight, had come down.

"I was going to fix it," I said. "Her parents. The staffing. I've got—"

"I know," said Ray. "You've got a thing."

"I've always got a thing."

"Hand me that."

I handed it to him. He took a second towel from a drawer and stood next to me, close enough that our elbows touched, and looked at the window.

"What I'd say," said Ray, "if I were saying anything, which I'm not, is that it's a nice thing to stand next to somebody with nothing to fix."

I didn't say anything. I was holding a plate. The water ran.

I want to tell you I understood it then. I'd had a warm, open feeling in a kitchen, and I took it home, and somewhere on the drive down the mountain it curdled, the way good feelings do, into a plan.

---

I built the spreadsheet on Sunday. It took me four hours.

It was a beauty. It was called **Raman Pharmacy: Staffing Options,** and it had a tab for each of three plans: a part-time technician, a cross-training schedule, and a rota of relatives, in which I'd included Arjun, whose availability I'd estimated at *weekends, guilt-adjusted.* It had a chart, and a column for *risk,* and at the bottom, in a bordered cell, a recommendation, in bold.

I emailed it to Maya at 11:08 p.m., from the floor of the new living room, with my back against the ugly good armchair, while she was in the shower. I attached a note. *Thought this might help. No pressure. — N.* I've kept the email. I find it hard to read.

She read it in the morning, standing at the counter, in her coat, with a cup of tea going cold. She read it for a long time. Then she closed the laptop, and looked at me, across the boxes that still covered the floor, and said, in a voice as level as a table, "That's thoughtful."

I thought I'd done something right.

It took me three weeks to learn that *thoughtful* was a door. That morning I stood in the kitchen with a cup, feeling a small warm glow of accomplishment, while a woman I loved took her bag from the hook, kissed me on the cheek, and left for work.

I sat down with my phone. I'd had an idea on the way to the kitchen, and I typed it in with real care. A recurring reminder, the sort of thing I'd built a company around: a small, repeating, gentle tap. Sundays at seven. Repeat forever.

**Maya check-in. Ask open questions. Listen. Don't fix.**

I looked at it. I was proud. I thought, *I've learned something.* I saved it, and went to work, and the reminder sat in the dark of my pocket like a small, well-meaning bomb for three Sundays.

---

**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Don't build what nobody asked for.*

Note to self.

The reminder I set on my phone, Sundays at seven:

*Maya check-in. Ask open questions. Listen. Don't fix.*

The version I should have set:

*Did she ask for this?*

At work I know the rule. Eight months on an inventory tool nobody asked for. Theo found the bug I'd spent a week on, in an afternoon, and waited to see whether I'd ask him for help.

At home I broke it inside a month. A row in the lease spreadsheet that said *Maya (50%).* A staffing model for her family's pharmacy that nobody had asked for. She said, "That's thoughtful," which is a door closing. Then the reminder, which was the inventory tool with a person as the customer.

Before I build anything for Maya or her family (a spreadsheet, a reminder, a plan), I ask whether she asked for it. If I can't say yes, I don't build it. Kyle asked before he advised, and it took him thirteen days to give away an armchair. I can wait thirteen minutes.
