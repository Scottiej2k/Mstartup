# Chapter 24
## Runway, Revisited

On the sixteenth of September, a Saturday, I drew the next thing on a napkin and didn't finish it.

I was at the kitchen table in Sunnyvale at a quarter to eight, with coffee, and the pen from the cup. Maya was still in bed. It was the first morning in a month when I had nowhere to be, and I'd been given, in the way these things happen, an idea. It came whole, as they do. I'd stopped giving myself credit for them.

It was a check-in for people who live alone.

I drew a circle, and in it I wrote *4B,* and next to it, a second circle: *Peralta.* Two men, neither of whom knew I'd ever heard of them: one found on a kitchen floor after two days, one who'd simply moved away to be near his daughter and left nobody knowing why. Neither had had a somebody. I drew a line from each to a smaller circle. *Who would you want to know?* I drew the backup circle, the one for when the first couldn't. I drew a box for the person at the pharmacy with an hour of her own. I was, I could see, about to draw the arrow, the one that goes back to the man, the one that says *someone has it,* and I had the pen on the paper, and I had begun it, a single line, down and to the right—

I stopped.

Nothing was wrong with the drawing. It was a good drawing, and it was mine, and there was a whole company to build behind it. I'd been waiting, I realized, for about a year, for the next idea to arrive so that I could feel like myself again. I held the pen. Down the hall the bedroom door was shut, and behind it a woman I loved was asleep, at a quarter to eight, on a Saturday, which was something she'd begun to do.

I put the cap on the pen. The arrow stopped halfway to the man. I left it there. It's still there. It's on the refrigerator, under a magnet, next to an envelope with a timeline on the back.

---

I should say what I'd noticed.

I'd noticed it in pieces. It's a thing I do now; I can't help it. I'd noticed first the coffee, at Ray's, on a Tuesday in the middle of August. Ray's coffee had been drinkable since the spring, which he considered a personal victory, and he'd poured her a cup, and she'd put her hand over it without looking and asked for water. She was not a coffee drinker. She was a tea person, with a mug in her hand every morning of her adult life, the way another person has a face. But she liked the *smell* of mine, she'd always said, and in the second week of September she began to leave the kitchen when I made it. She said it smelled *loud.* She said it with her hand over her nose, quite calmly, as if reporting a fact about the weather. I asked her about it once, lightly, at the counter: *you okay?* She said the smell was loud, and she'd been sleeping badly, and she was fine. It was a reasonable answer. Probably it was even true, as far as it went. I decided, on purpose, to take it.

I noticed a banana in her bag. I noticed a nap, on a Tuesday, at four, on the couch in the room behind the pharmacy, with her white coat folded under her head. I noticed that she'd turned down a second helping, at her mother's table. Nobody in the Raman family has ever turned down a second helping. Lakshmi had noticed, too. I'd seen her notice, across the table, and say nothing, and write nothing, and put a second portion in a container for Maya to take home.

I noticed that on Saturdays she slept until nine. I counted six things. I did not add them.

I'd learned, in a kitchen in February, with an envelope, what I was supposed to do about a thing like that. It was the thing I'd been told, and had agreed to, and had written on the refrigerator. *Don't fix. Just be the one I tell.* That's the part about the telling, and it has a second part, which nobody mentions, which is that you have to leave room for it. You can't go in and get it. It's her news, and she gets to decide when it becomes ours. I'd asked once and been answered, and that was the end of my part. You stand in a doorway, and you wait to be told.

It was the hardest thing I did that year. I'd built a company out of the idea that you notice, and tell a person, and I stood in my own kitchen with six pieces of evidence and let her keep them. I caught myself, on the eighteenth, in the bathroom mirror, rehearsing the face I'd make. It was a good face. It was *surprised.* Then I heard it, over the faucet, and I stopped, and washed my hands, and went back out.

---

Ms. Adeyemi had called on the twelfth, a Tuesday, as she'd said she would. I'd been ready for the call for a week, and it was nothing like what I'd prepared.

"You said September," she said.

"I did."

"I'm asking again whether I should ask again." She said it in the dry voice. "You've had your six months. Is it yours to take?"

I looked at the window, at the parking lot, at a woman getting out of a car with a toddler on her hip.

"Yes," I said. "In the spring, in one hospital. Yours, if you'll have us. With three conditions. The mother presses the button herself, in her own bed, before she leaves, and she names the people, and they say yes. Somebody on your floor is the named person for the floor, with an hour, and cover. And you tell me, in the first month, every time it's wrong."

There was a silence on the line. Then she said, "That's the first yes I've gotten that came with homework."

"It's not a yes without it."

"I'll send you a date," said Ms. Adeyemi. "Thank you, Mr. Calloway." And then, in a different tone, quieter: "I hope it's for someone you know."

I didn't understand that sentence. I thought it was a politeness.

---

I bought the word on the twentieth.

It had taken three weeks and a series of emails between Priya and a woman named Mrs. Halvorsen, who ran Fjellvik Footwear, of Bergen, Norway, from an office I pictured as having a window onto a harbor. She'd written, in the first week, that the domain *loopback.com* had been registered by her father in 1999 for a line of boots which had been discontinued, and that they'd had no use for it in twenty years, but that it was *a matter of principle not to sell a name to a person who doesn't understand it.* Priya had written back four paragraphs. Mrs. Halvorsen had replied with three words. *Tell me more.*

I got on a call. I told her about a man on a floor, and a woman named Lena whose phone had been in a locker, and a card in a pharmacy bag, and a word on a whiteboard, written twice, in marker. She listened. She didn't say anything for some time, and in the background I could hear a clock.

"My father," said Mrs. Halvorsen, "would say it is a good boot." She paused. "It's an expensive boot. Thirty-eight thousand dollars."

"Done."

"You don't wish to bargain?"

"No," I said. "I think I've done the bargaining."

At 4:10, the transfer went through. Priya sat across the glass table with her laptop, and turned it toward me, and on the screen was a browser, and in the browser was the word. I'd typed it ten thousand times. I hit enter and it loaded a page with a gray box, a sentence, and a form. Priya had written the sentence. It said, *Is anyone there?*

"It's a lot of money for a word," said Priya. (The round had closed in June. She says that's the only reason she let me.)

"It's the word we started with."

"I know." She looked at it, and her voice dropped, as it does perhaps twice a year. "We didn't have a company then. We had a word and a whiteboard and a Norwegian shoe company." She closed the laptop. "Come on. Kyle has made a cake. It's in the shape of a *boot.*"

It had a very detailed lace. Divya had done the lace.

---

Ray called on the twenty-first, a Thursday, at about the time he calls.

"It's me," said Ray. "I'm going to ask you something and I'd like you not to do anything about it."

"All right."

"There's been no answer." He didn't say from whom. "It'll be a year on Tuesday. I've written one every year since the sale, and it's the first I ever sent. I check the box. I know what it means when a man in his seventies writes a letter and doesn't hear back. It's nothing." The pump ran, behind him, in the pond. "I'm saying it aloud because it's the thing I'd have told nobody."

"Do you want me to say something?"

"No." A long pause. "I want you to know it. That's all."

I stood in the doorway of the bedroom. Maya was asleep, again, in the middle of a Thursday evening, with her hand under her cheek.

"I know it," I said. "Thank you for telling me."

"Mm," said Ray. "There's another thing. A nurse called me last night. A night nurse, from Daly City. She'd seen a notice about the fund. She asked if she could apply." He cleared his throat. "I said yes. I said she could come to the house. There's going to be a first one, on the ninth of October. A meeting." He stopped, again. "Denise would have put her in charge of the dishes."

"I'll come."

"I thought you might," said Ray. And hung up, without goodbye.

---

On Monday, the twenty-fifth, we had been married one year.

I did not say *cliff.* It was in my mouth at about five in the afternoon, in the car, going up the 101: I thought, *there's a word for a year, in my business, the day someone has stayed long enough that it's theirs.* I got as far as the *c.* I could feel it, a small, eager thing, with a slide behind it. I closed my mouth and changed lanes.

We went to the dumpling place with the fish tank.

It hadn't changed. There was the tank, with the plastic castle, and a fish going in and out of the castle as if trying to remember something. There was the same waitress, with the same expression, who looked at Maya for a good three seconds, and at me for rather longer, and said, "Ah." She brought hot tea in two cups and a plate of dumplings we hadn't ordered, and she didn't write anything down.

We ate. Maya had water, and not the tea, and she ate the dumplings with her usual patience, and left the ones with the vinegar. We talked about the pharmacy, and the rota, and Theo's two days, and Priya, and whether Kyle had said anything to Divya's parents, which Kyle had not. We talked about nothing in particular. At about eight, in the lull that comes after the plates are taken, I put my hand in my back pocket and took out my wallet.

"It's tonight, isn't it?" said Maya. She'd said it in the spring, over the dishes: *at the first anniversary, if we still want to.* "The napkins."

"Do we still want to?"

"Yes," said Maya.

I opened it. Behind the driver's license, behind a card with four questions on it, was a very small square of paper. It had been there for nearly three years. It had gone soft at the folds, and gray, and one corner had a faint ring from a glass of water. I laid it on the table between us.

She had her bag on the seat beside her. She took out a small zippered pouch, and out of the pouch a square, the same size, a little less gray. It had been, she said, in the pocket of every coat she'd owned, and in the pouch when she wasn't wearing one. She set it next to mine.

We looked at them.

"Together?" said Maya.

"You first."

"No. Together."

We unfolded them at the same time. It takes a long time, to unfold a paper that's been folded into a postage stamp for nearly three years. The folds fight you. I got mine open on the fourth try. It was in my handwriting, the hurried one, the one I use for notes to myself, in the blue pen she'd put on top of it, a little smeared.

*1. Priya.*
*2. Don't be the reason anyone's waiting.*
*3. Maya. Before the company.*

I read it, in a booth, on the first anniversary of my wedding, and felt a deep, ordinary shame in the back of the neck. I'd said, in the middle of this book, that I'd tell you what it said when I'd earned the right. I hadn't earned it. She read it anyway.

"I wrote the third one in December," I said. "Two years before the wedding. I thought it was true." I heard my voice. "I spent the next two years finding out I hadn't meant it yet."

"I know," said Maya.

"You *know?*"

"I've known where you keep it since the spring. It's got a *ring* on it." She almost smiled. She had hers flat in front of her, in her small neat hand. "You wrote it down, though. That's more than most people manage." She turned the napkin around on the table, and pushed it, with the flat of her finger, to my side.

*1. My mother's list.*
*2. My name on my door.*
*3. To say it myself.*

It was shorter than I'd imagined. I had pictured, for three years, a document. A treaty, a charter, the full text of a woman. I read the three lines. I read the third one twice.

"I wrote it in a restaurant," said Maya, "at the end of a bad month, in a coat, to a man who kept telling me things I hadn't asked. And I thought, *this one will either listen or he won't.*" She looked at the table. "I wrote number two because I thought I'd lose it. Raman is the name on the sign. I thought it would come up. You never said it."

"I never thought it."

"I know. I found that out around the second Tuesday." She tapped the third line, with one finger. "I kept waiting to be asked what I wanted, and then to be left to say it. And then you asked me what I wanted to happen, at a table, in July, and didn't speak, and I realized it was the whole napkin."

I couldn't say anything. I looked at the fish going in and out of the castle.

"There's a fourth," said Maya.

"A fourth what?"

She'd taken out of her bag a thing I knew: a pencil, about two inches long, sharpened with a kitchen knife, with a flat brown end where a thumb had been, and on the barrel, the remains of a name in worn gold. She held it as one would hold a very small bird. "I used this. This morning. On the back." She picked up her napkin, and without turning it over, put it into the pocket of her coat, which she had, I saw, brought after all, and which hung on the hook by the door. "I'm not ready for you to read it yet. It's—" She stopped. "Can you wait?"

I looked at her. I looked at the napkin, the small gray corner of it, in the pocket of the coat. I'd had six pieces of evidence for five weeks.

"Yes," I said.

"I mean it. You'll have to *wait.*"

"I know how."

"You don't," said Maya, gently. "But you're good at it."

---

I woke at ten to six.

It's our hour, the six o'clock, which began in February, the morning after the envelope. Neither of us had planned it. We woke at about the same time, before the alarms and the phones, in the blue light, with the radiator ticking. We lay there, and told the truth, in the dark, in small amounts. Mostly it was a thing we did with our eyes closed.

It was a Tuesday. I noticed that. It was the twenty-sixth of September. I lay on my back with the ceiling gone gray and heard the sound of the refrigerator and the bird that lives in the lot, and beside me, the sound of Maya not sleeping.

She got up. I heard her go to the kitchen. I heard water run, and a glass set down. I lay there for a minute, and then I got up, too, and stood in the door of the bedroom in a t-shirt and watched her stand at the counter in the dim kitchen with a glass of water in her hand and her coat over her arm.

She'd brought it in from the hook. It was the coat she keeps in the car; she'd carried it up the night before. She'd taken something out of the pocket. It was a small, soft, gray square, refolded once, and she held it in the hand that had no glass.

"I've been up since four," said Maya.

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"I heard you. I didn't—" I stopped. "You said to wait."

"I did." She turned. In the light from the window over the sink, her face had an expression I'd never seen on it, and I think it's the one she'll have whenever she's said the thing she's afraid of. It was not frightened. It was the face of a person standing at the edge of a bright, ordinary room. "I wrote it with my mother's pencil. I thought it'd be a way to say it that was mine. And then I found I couldn't hold it out."

She held it out.

I crossed the kitchen. I took the napkin out of her hand, which was cold. I turned it over. On the back, in pencil, in small, careful capitals, in the hand of a woman who writes the doses on a bottle:

*4. ELEVEN WEEKS. APRIL.*

I read it. I read it again.

April. I did the arithmetic, which I've never been able to stop doing. Seven months of runway, and for once in my life I wanted to be on it. And then I thought nothing. I stood in a kitchen with a paper in my hand that weighed less than a coin, and my mind did what it does when a very large number goes through it, which is: it was quiet. Then it wasn't. Something came up through me from about the knees, and I understood that it was not surprise. I'd known. I'd had six things on a list, and I'd waited five weeks, and I'd practiced my face in a mirror, and none of it had been any use whatever.

"Maya," I said.

She was watching me. She had both hands around the glass.

"I wanted to tell you at dinner," she said, "and I couldn't. I thought if I said it in the restaurant, it'd be a thing we *did.* An occasion. I wanted to say it on a Tuesday." She laughed, once, through her nose, and put her hand up, and took it away. "I'm so frightened. I'm so—Nate, I'm so *happy,* and it's all at once, and I don't know which one's the real one, and I'm sorry, I'm not doing this—"

"Don't be sorry."

"I wrote *nothing urgent* to you once," said Maya. "And I'm telling you, now. It's very urgent."

I didn't say anything smart. There was nothing at all in my head, and for once it wasn't a failure of the machine. I put the napkin down on the counter. I went to her. I was about to take the glass; I'd got one hand out for it, a reflex, the old one, the one that fixes things. She was unsteady. She'd had no sleep. The water was shaking a little in the light.

She looked at my hand. She looked at me.

"I've got it," said Maya.

I took my hand back. I stood there, with it at my side, empty. She drank. She put the glass on the counter, very carefully, in the middle of the dark wood, and wiped her mouth with her wrist, and then she stepped forward and put her forehead against my collarbone, and I put my arms around her, with nothing in them but her. The refrigerator hummed. Out in the lot, the bird started up again, and in the dark of the apartment, on the table in the other room, my phone lit once, and buzzed, against the wood.

I knew what it was. It was six o'clock. Since the hospital, Suresh had sent me a single letter every morning, in addition to the machine, in case the machine was wrong. I knew it said *A.*

I didn't go to look.

"Okay?" I said, into her hair.

"Okay," said Maya.

There was nothing around it.

**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Notice, ask once, then let her tell it.*

Note to self, and to you, whoever you turn out to be.

At work, noticing is the whole product. Loopback notices when someone goes quiet and tells the person they chose. I've spent three years getting good at it: the second ring, the named person, the text that says someone has it. And the first rule of the product is the one I almost forgot at home: the person presses the button herself. We never told anyone anything she hadn't agreed to.

At home I noticed six things in five weeks, and I did the one thing the product allows. I asked once, lightly. *You okay?* Maya gave me an answer that made sense: the coffee smelled loud, she'd been sleeping badly. It was plausible. It was probably even true, as far as it went. And I decided, on purpose, to take it. That was the hard part. Noticing is easy; I'm built for it. Choosing to believe a reasonable answer, when some part of me suspects a bigger one, is respect. She was allowed to have something that was hers before it was ours. I wasn't owed it on my schedule.

Rule: notice, ask once, accept the answer, and leave a door open on my side. If it's something she wants me to know, she'll say it. If I go in and get it, I've taken the one thing she gets to give.

I've been rehearsing what I want you to know about me. You'll never meet the first version of me, the one with the spreadsheet and the funnel and the joke about a cliff. I shipped that one to your mother, and she was kind enough to use it anyway. Everything in this book is what I learned by being wrong in front of people who loved me. I hope it was enough. I'm going to be the dad who asks the second question and waits. The one who answers on the first ring, and who loves you, and says so before you ask.
