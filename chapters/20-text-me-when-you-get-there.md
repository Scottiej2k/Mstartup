# Chapter 20
## Text Me When You Get There

I gave Priya the key at ten o'clock on Friday night, in the glass room, with the lights off.

There was one key. It was small and black, on a green lanyard, and it opened nothing in the physical world. It opened a door in a computer: the pause switch for all forty stores. On a screen on the wall of the glass room there were forty rows. Green meant nobody had flagged anything. Red meant somebody had. She stood on the other side of the table in the dark, with the fish sign glowing faintly in the hall, and I held it out to her across the wood.

"It's yours," I said. "Tomorrow, at nine. I'll be at City Hall. I'll turn my phone off."

"You don't have to turn it off."

"I know." I'd practiced the next part in the car. "If I leave it on, I'll look. If I look, I'll help. And then it's mine, on the morning you'd have been the one."

She took it. "There's a runbook," I said. (The manual for the worst night.) "Ines wrote it."

"I've read it. I made her cut it from twenty." She hung the lanyard around her neck, over her sweater, with a small, formal motion, as if accepting a medal. "Nate. Go get married."

"What if it breaks?"

"Then it breaks, and I answer." She said it without any weight. "That's the whole company. Go."

I got as far as the door. Under the fish sign in the hall there was a fourth line, in a fourth marker, new, in a very small hand. **I DON'T EVEN LIKE FISH. — D.**

I laughed, once, in the dark.

"Since Tuesday," said Priya, behind me, not turning. "Third floor. Accounting software. He's been reading that sign for two years."

"Nate."

I turned. She hadn't moved. She was standing in the dark with the key against her sweater, looking at the forty rows.

"The cousin," said Priya. "In the car. I said it didn't matter."

"You don't have to—"

"I'd like to. I'd like somebody to have it." She didn't sit. "Her name was Meera. She was nine years older than me, and the funniest person at every wedding I went to until I was twenty. She married a man who was good at being liked. It took her six years to leave, and she did it the way you're supposed to, with a friend, in a week, with a new phone and a new number. She told four people. I was one of the four." She looked at the key. "For eleven months he didn't find her. Then somebody in the family, who loved her, who was so happy she was safe, added her new number to a group chat for a baby shower. Two dozen people. Nobody was careless, exactly. Everyone was just being kind."

I didn't say anything.

"He texted her the next day. It was a joke, about a restaurant they'd been to. That's what made it so bad. It was a joke." Her voice was entirely level. "She moved again. She stopped going to weddings. I haven't seen her in nine years."

"Priya."

"That's why the rule's first," she said. "A message that arrives isn't neutral. It tells somebody where somebody is, even if it says nothing at all. That's why the person presses the button herself. That's why the footer has no name." She lifted the lanyard a little, and let it fall. "It's why this has a pause. If the rows go red tomorrow I won't be fixing anything. I'll be deciding who gets a text. I need to be able to stop it, and I need to know I can."

"You can."

"I know I can," said Priya. "That's why I wanted to say it out loud. Go get married."

I turned the phone off in the car, at 10:22, and watched the screen go dark. I sat there for some time, with a black rectangle in my palm.

I should say what happened on the Friday, because it was the first of the two days, and because I nearly missed it by being nervous about the second.

Maya cooked with her mother, the way she'd asked: the kitchen, the day before, with the door shut. I was given a cup of tea and a magazine about pill counters, and I heard through the wall, for four hours, a sound I'd never heard in that house, which was two women not saying anything while they worked. I looked in once. Lakshmi stood at the stove with a small steel dish of spices, and she held it out, and Maya took a pinch, and put it in. She didn't say *Amma.* Lakshmi didn't say *no.* Lakshmi nodded, once, and turned back to the pot, and I understood that I'd just seen a woman be allowed, at twenty-nine, to season.

The ceremony was at four, small, as she'd asked: twenty-one people on folding chairs, a low brass lamp, and a priest from the temple with a bicycle helmet at his feet, who explained in English the parts I needed. Maya sat. That was the point. She sat on a low stool in a marigold sari that had been her grandmother's, with her eyes on the flame, for the whole hour, and nobody asked her to get up and carry anything. I watched Lakshmi watch her. It was a long, complicated face, and I read it then: *I didn't know it would be this easy. I should have asked.*

Afterward, in the kitchen doorway, with a paper plate, an aunt of Maya's whose name I'd been told twice and had lost both times looked me up and down.

"You are not Tamil," she said.

"Ohio."

"Is that near?"

"It's in the other direction."

She ate something. "Maya could have married a doctor."

"I'm told there are several."

She considered that. "You are honest," she said, as if reporting a symptom. Behind her, Lakshmi, whom I'd been braced for, said something short in Tamil, and the aunt said, "Aiyo," and moved on with her plate. Maya told me later that it was *He's the one who asked.* She wouldn't translate the rest.

I met her brother on the back step.

He was sitting at the top of the steps in a rumpled blue shirt, with a plate on his knee that he wasn't eating: a tall, tired man of thirty-three, with Maya's eyes and Suresh's jaw. Arjun. He'd flown in from Seattle that morning, from a twenty-four-hour shift.

"You're the founder," said Arjun.

"I'm the groom."

"Same thing, in this family." He moved over. I sat. We looked at the lemon tree. "I should tell you I wasn't a fan. Not of you. Of the idea. She's had a store she was supposed to leave for four years and a store she was supposed to inherit, and then a man with a startup walks in with a spreadsheet and feelings about *systems.* I looked you up. Loopback sounds like a pretzel." He ate nothing off the plate. "She said you asked my father for his permission. He told me, in the car. He said it twice. I think it's the longest conversation he's had about a person in a decade."

I laughed, and he did, a short, exhausted bark.

"I've worked emergency rooms for six years," said Arjun. "I see people who've sat in a kitchen for three days with something in their chest because they didn't want to be a *bother.* We don't ask anyone for help, in this family. It's the only skill my father taught us. I called her every Sunday for six years, and I never once asked if she was all right, because I knew what she'd say."

"*I'm fine.*"

"*I'm fine, Arjun.*" He nodded slowly. "She's not. She's been carrying the store since she was nine. I left, and she stayed, and I let her." He looked up. "I'm not telling you to fix it. I'm telling you to be the one she tells."

"Somebody said that to me already," I said. "Not in those words."

"Then it's two of us." He stood up, stiffly, and held out his free hand. I shook it. "Welcome to the family. I'm still watching you. Don't hold the door for anyone. They'll be offended."

On Saturday morning, my father gave me a toolbox.

It was ten to nine, in a small paneled room off the rotunda at City Hall that smelled of floor wax, where a card on the door said **PARTY.** I was in a gray suit my mother had had altered over a video call, sitting on a bench, with my phone off in my breast pocket, feeling its small dead weight against my ribs. I'd never been without it at nine in the morning on a day when anything could break.

The door opened. It was my father, in a navy suit a little tight across the shoulders, holding something by the handle in a hand too large for it. He didn't say hello. He sat on the bench, about two feet from me, and put it down on the floor between us.

It was a small red steel toolbox, the one from his garage, with a dent on the lid shaped like a thumb and **DAN** on the handle in old marker. It had been, in the house I grew up in, the single most reliable object.

"Open it," said my father.

I undid the clasps. Inside, each in its worn slot, were his screwdriver and his level and a tape measure. Lying across them was an index card, and on it, in my father's block capitals, in ballpoint, were six words.

**TEXT ME WHEN YOU GET THERE.**

I looked at it for a long time.

"I've been saying it wrong," said my father. "I say it at the airport. Every time. I say it to the wall." He looked at his hands, big and still on his knees, like two tools put down. "Annie told me it sounds like you're checking. And I said, well, I am. It means I want to know you're all right. It's the only way I know to say that I want to know. I wanted you to have the box. It's the only thing I've got that's any use."

"Where's *there?*" I said. I'd meant it as a joke. It didn't come out as one.

My father looked at me. It was the look from the curb, at an airport, a year and a half earlier.

"Wherever it is," said Dan Calloway. "After."

I put the card in my breast pocket, against the phone. I held out my hand, and he took it in both of his, and we sat there on a bench, two men who fix things and don't discuss them, not discussing it. At 9:00 I was holding my father's hand in a room with a card on the door, and I didn't know what was happening at forty pharmacies. I thought about it, and then I thought about something else.

---

The officiant was Winston.

I'd asked. I'd telephoned the office the day after the rehearsal and said, *is it possible to request an officiant?* and he'd said, in his dry, courteous voice, *you'd like me,* and I'd said yes, and he'd said, *I've been marrying people for thirty-one years. I've never been requested.* And then, after a pause, *I'd be honored.*

He stood at the top of the white stairs, under the dome, in a gray suit and a mustache and a lanyard, with a small black book in one hand, in the gold light, and the rotunda was full.

There were twenty-three of us, which was Maya's forty with the cousins cut, standing in a loose half-circle on the marble with the sound of our shoes going up into the dome and coming back. Suresh stood in a dark suit with the face of a man who's decided in advance not to be moved. My mother held my father's arm. Ray stood a little apart, by a pillar, looking at the dome as though checking it for load. Kyle, in the back, held up a speaker like a bomb.

And there were people I hadn't expected. Dr. Okafor, in a lilac cardigan, looking at the ceiling with mild clinical interest. Mr. Alvarez, in a wheelchair, with his daughter Gloria behind him and a lollipop in his pocket; he'd come against the advice of two doctors, because he'd been missed once and he'd be damned if he'd miss this. Mrs. Chen, and her grandson Kevin, in a tie, with the stricken look of a young man who'd been told to hold a purse. He had a handkerchief ready for his grandmother and used it twice, and afterward I saw him stay behind to fold the programs.

Priya stood on my left, in a dark blue dress, with the lanyard still round her neck. Jules stood on Maya's right, with the expression of someone who hasn't decided whether to cry.

And Maya came in.

I'll tell you how she came in, because I've told it eleven times and it's always the same. She came in from the side, through a small door under the stairs, without music, without anyone to give her away. She walked out onto the marble alone, in a green and gold sari, with her hair in a low braid and a yellow flower pinned at the end of it, and her hands loose at her sides. She wasn't looking at anyone. She was looking at the staircase. And then, about halfway, she looked at me.

I've been looked at by her in a ballroom, in a coffee shop, through four inches of door. I'd never been looked at by her with nothing in the way: no chain, no coat, no window, no polite. I remember that I stopped breathing, and I remember that I didn't mind.

She came across the marble to the foot of the stairs, where I stood on a small brass plate, and took her place beside me. Winston stood on the landing above us, in the gold light.

"It's a good echo," said Winston gently, to both of us.

"We know," said Maya.

"I have the paperwork here," said Winston, to the dome. "Maya Raman, who has told me twice, by phone, that she's keeping it. And Nathan Calloway, who has not argued." He turned a page. "I want it noted that nobody has argued."

"Noted," said Maya.

---

In a civil ceremony there's a point where the officiant asks if you have words. They don't need them. They're allowed. Winston said it in a low, kind voice, to the two of us, with the black book open on his palm: "Do you have anything you'd like to say?"

I hadn't written anything. I'd decided not to, and had been, for a week, in cold terror about it.

"I don't have a backup," I said.

Winston looked at me over the book.

"That's not in the book," he said.

"It's in ours," said Maya.

She said it quietly. She turned to me, on the landing, with the whole room behind her, and put her hand in mine. It was small and warm and dry. Her hand was bare. The bands were in Winston's breast pocket; she'd picked them in the spring, from a jeweler on Valencia.

"I choose you," said Maya. "And I'll tell you when I'm scared. Not by being polite." She stopped. Her voice shook, and steadied. "And you'll answer."

"I'll answer," I said.

"That's all."

"That's all I've got."

Winston took the two plain bands from his pocket, and we put them on each other's hands, hers steady, mine not.

He closed the book on his thumb. He looked at us for a moment, and at the room, and I saw his mouth move under the mustache.

"Then by the authority of the City and County of San Francisco," he said, "I'd like to say that I've done this thirty-one years, and that was the fastest and best one." He took a breath. "You're married. Take your time."

We kissed. Maya started it, which is one of the few facts about our marriage I'm sure of. It went on long enough that the room gave up and applauded, and kept applauding, and then began to look at the ceiling. Winston checked his watch. He has since denied this. There is a photograph.

It was somewhere in the noise that Lakshmi came up the steps. I hadn't seen her move. I saw her, suddenly, in a good green sari with her glasses pushed up into her hair, standing in front of her daughter on the landing, a head shorter, straight. She didn't say anything. She took Maya's right hand, and turned it over, and pressed something into the palm, and folded the fingers closed over it with both of her own.

Maya looked down. She looked at her closed hand, and then at her mother, and I saw her mouth open, and nothing come out.

"Later," said Lakshmi, quietly. "Not now. Later." She patted the hand, twice, and stepped back, and put her glasses on.

I never saw what it was, until the evening. I'll tell you when.

I turned on the phone in the rotunda, on the steps, in the sun, with a petal in my hair.

I'd planned to wait until the party. But we came out through the big doors onto the plaza, into a cold, bright, windy noon, with twenty-three people throwing rice at us, or, in the Raman family's case, flowers, and somewhere in the noise my hand went to my pocket on its own. I held the button down. The phone began, in my palm, to shake.

There were forty-two notifications. None was from Meridian. None was from Carla. Thirty-nine were from a group chat, which turned out to be my sister, and my mother, and Arjun, who had been standing eleven feet from me. One was from Kyle.

Two were from Priya. The first had arrived at 9:14 in the morning, in a plain gray bubble, with no greeting and no sticker.

**They're all green.**

And beneath it, one minute later, a second:

**Go away.**

I read them twice, on the steps of City Hall, with my wife's hand in mine. It was closer to what you feel when you've thrown something a long way and it's landed.

"What is it?" said Maya.

I showed her.

She read it. She looked at me, and at the phone, and she began, in the sun, with rice in her hair, to laugh, helplessly, with her hand over her mouth.

"*Go away,*" said Maya. "She told you to go away. At nine. In writing."

"I know."

"You didn't."

"I turned it off."

"You *did,*" said Maya. She put her head against my arm. "That's the best present you could've given her."

The party was in Ray's yard, from four until it was too dark to see.

There was a canvas awning at the top of the lawn, and long trestle tables under it, and on them, in steel pots and on banana leaves, in a smell I could have navigated by, the food. The Ramans' cousins had come in vans from Fremont and Sunnyvale and Fresno, and had taken over Ray's kitchen at noon without being asked. Strings of small white lights ran from the oak to the shed to the fence.

And there was a pond. It was finished: a wide, irregular kidney of dark water, with a ring of flat gray stones and a bench on a shallow ledge, and a single lily pad that one of Denise's cousins had brought in a jar. It had been, at the beginning, a curved line of string.

Ray was standing at the edge of it in his jacket with a mug of coffee when I went down.

"You dug it," I said.

"I had help," said Ray. He didn't turn his head. "Dan came up in October, last year. He didn't ask. I never learned who told him. He arrived with a shovel and a thermos and started in on the east side." He took a sip. "We didn't say anything for nine days."

My mother, it later emerged, was the one who'd told him, and had kept that secret for eleven months.

"It was the best nine days I've had since Denise," said Ray.

We sat on the bench on the ledge, with the water at our feet. Behind us the party went on in its sprawling, joyful way, with a cousin shouting something in Tamil about rice. He took off his glasses and set them on his knee.

"I'm going to tell you what it is," he said. "It's a fellowship, though the paperwork says *Scholarship Fund.* The Denise Calloway Scholarship Fund. I've given to the hospitals for years, the usual. This is different. It's for night nurses, on the pediatric wards to start. A year's pay for a nurse who wants to stay on nights, and to spend part of every shift sitting with the ones who are frightened. It pays for the hours."

"There was a woman," said Ray. "Her name was Bernadette. She was the night nurse on the oncology floor, in the spring, the last three months. She was fifty-eight. She had eight patients on a busy night." He looked at the mug on his knee. "She came in one night at three. Denise was awake. Bernadette looked at her, from the door, for about a second, and came in, and sat down on the edge of the bed, and didn't do anything. She didn't check the pump. She didn't chart. She sat there for forty minutes. In the morning Denise told me she'd been terrified, and nobody had known, and then someone had. She said *she saw it from the door.*" He put his glasses on. "I looked up the schedule, afterward. Bernadette wasn't supposed to be on the floor at three. She'd made it hers."

"Nobody's job was to notice," said Ray. "She made it hers. I'd like to give someone the forty minutes." He looked at me, finally, a long, pale, level look. "You said *something* to me once, about healthcare. I thought, I'm too old. I've got a hole in a lawn. And then you kept coming up the mountain with people who'd gone quiet, and I thought, well. There's a something."

"You asked me about nurses," I said. "In May."

"I wanted it to work." He said it without embarrassment. "You're a good boy not to ask. Never ask me why I didn't say. It's your wedding. Drink something."

I took his hand. I don't think I'd ever done it. It was large and dry, with a finger that didn't bend, and it didn't pull away. We sat by the pond he'd dug, and neither of us said another word.

---

I'll give you the rest as it came.

Kyle gave his toast at the worst possible moment. It was a quarter to six, when the cousins were bringing out the sweets, and my mother and Lakshmi had just, for the first time, been allowed to stand together at a trestle table, arranging something, in an atmosphere of delicate truce. He tapped a glass with a fork. He cleared his throat. "I'd like to say a few words," said Kyle, in the tone of a man about to be extremely late. "About the second person."

There was a silence.

"I used to be the first," said Kyle. He'd taken out a card. It was an index card, I saw, and I felt a small, cold dread. "For twelve weeks. And then Nate got a better one. And I thought, well, that's it. I'm the backup." His voice cracked. "And then I realized what a backup is. It's the one who's there. It's the person who comes with a hand truck. And I'd like to say, to the two of you—" He stopped. He looked at the card. "I forgot the rest. I wrote it in a car. I'll just say: to the second person. Thank you for the cereal." He raised his glass. "I'm going to cry now."

He did. He stood at the head of a trestle table, in a tie, and wept, with great sincerity, while my sister, from the back, called out, "Kyle, sit *down,* you're ruining the sweets," and Jules, beside her, put a handkerchief in his hand. It was the best toast I've ever heard. I'd say that to anyone. It was the worst-timed in recorded history, and I wouldn't have changed a word.

Cole came alone, in the jacket the color of wet sand, and stood at the far side of the pond with a plate he wasn't eating. "I wanted to see what it looked like," he said. "A lot of people holding plates." He smiled, and it was a good smile, and it went out. "Optionality's a lonely word on a Saturday, Nate." There was a seat at our table, I said. "I know," said Cole. "I'm going to sit in it. In a minute."

Priya sat on the stone ledge with her shoes off and her feet in the water. "They're all green," she said, when I sat down. "I told you to go away."

"I read that too."

"You went." She ate a sweet. "It's all I wanted."

Maya found me at dusk. She'd taken off her shoes, too. She came across the grass in her bare feet, in the green and gold, with her braid half undone, and sat on the stone ledge beside me, and for a while we watched the party the way you watch a river.

"How are you?" I said.

She thought about it. She actually thought about it, with her face turned toward the water. The string lights had come on, one by one, along the fence. A cousin of hers was laughing at something, up by the tables, with her head back.

"Tired," said Maya. "In a good way. Like when you put down something you've carried a long way and your arms don't know what to do."

"I'd say I understand," I said.

"But?"

"But I carried a laptop."

She laughed through her nose, and looked at the water. "That's honest."

"I'd like to know what it's like."

"You will." She looked at her hands. "It takes about a year."

She had something in her hand. She'd had it all day, in a loose fist, in a fold of her sari. She opened it, in the dark, between us, on her knee.

It was a pencil. It was about two inches long, a stub, sharpened with a kitchen knife, with a flat, brown, worn-smooth end where a thumb had been, and a little cap of teeth marks, and on the barrel, in worn gold, the remains of a name. I couldn't read it. It was the sort of pencil you get free from a hardware store.

"That's hers," said Maya. "It's the one from behind her ear. For thirty years. She wrote the roll with it. She wrote *Nate. Cup.*" She turned it over on her palm. "She said, *When you want it.*"

"Do you want it?"

"No." She said it quickly. And then, more slowly: "I don't want the list. I've had it since I was nine. She knows. That's why she gave it to me." She closed her fingers over the pencil. "It's for when I do."

I put my arm around her. Somewhere across the water, Kyle, who'd been given back his speaker, put on a song. I didn't know it. It was slow. It had, as I learned later, a title, a number of verses, and not one of the nine she hated.

I texted my father at 11:52 that night, from the guest room at Ray's, with the red toolbox on the chair.

The card was beside it, on the table. Maya had fallen asleep against the headboard before she'd finished taking out her pins. I held the phone. I thought of a wind, and a hand held out at an airport. I typed it with my thumbs.

*We got here.*

He's always asleep by ten. The reply came in under a minute. It was one word, in lowercase, without punctuation, in the flat grammar of a man who's learned to type with one finger.

*good*

I put the phone down, face up, by the card. I thought about *there.* I thought that I'd been looking for it my whole life in the wrong direction, and that it had been, all along, a place where a person could be asked.

I was up at six on Sunday, and I went out to the mailbox with Ray.

It was a gray, cold morning, with mist in the redwoods. He didn't say good morning. He stood up from the kitchen table, slowly, and went to the drawer with the batteries, and took out an envelope, a little gray at the edges, with a single word on the front in a careful block hand, and under it an address in Oregon.

"It's one page," said Ray. "It asks him to help with the fellowship. It isn't a speech."

He looked at me. I nodded.

We walked down the gravel drive together, in the mist, in silence. At the bottom, at the road, was the mailbox, on a post that had leaned two degrees to the left for as long as I'd known it.

He stopped at the box. His thumb moved on the paper, once, along the edge. He opened the little metal door, and put the letter in, and closed it, and put the flag up. That was all. I'd never seen a man do anything so hard so quietly.

There was a sound behind us on the gravel. It was my father, in his flannel shirt and work boots, with the red toolbox in one hand. He didn't look at either of us. He set the box down by the post and took out the level and held it against the wood, and looked at the bubble, and shook his head slightly. He took a shovel from where it had been leaning against the fence since last night, and dug for about four minutes at the foot of the post, with the quick, economical strokes of a man who has done it a thousand times, and then he put both hands on the post and straightened it. The bubble moved, and settled. He looked at the level, and then, for about a second, over the top of the post, at his brother.

Ray looked at him. My father and my uncle, who had talked about the weather at Christmas for thirty years, stood on either side of a mailbox post in the mist with a level between them, and it was an entire conversation, in a language the two of them had invented as boys, in Dayton, out of nouns.

"That'll hold," said my father.

"It will," said Ray.

**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Hand it over so you can be there.*

Note to self.

At work, the day that mattered was also the day that could break the company, and I couldn't be in both places. So on Friday night I gave Priya a small black key, the pause switch for forty stores, and a runbook written by someone else. Then I turned my phone off. The rule I gave her: if I leave it on, I'll look; if I look, I'll help; and then it's mine on the morning she should have had it.

At home, the same rule is what let me stand at City Hall at eleven o'clock and be married. Not half there with a phone in my pocket. Dan gave me a red toolbox with a card in it, *TEXT ME WHEN YOU GET THERE,* and I answered it, for the first time in my life, that night.

Before a day that matters to Maya: pick the person who holds the key, write down what they do, and turn the phone off. Then the company has to run without me, and I have to let it.
