# Chapter 20
## After the Confetti

At 3:07 on Tuesday morning, the third night after the launch, my phone lit up on the nightstand and showed me a row of text the color of a traffic cone.

*STORE 22 (GILROY): ENROLLMENT SYNC STALLED 14 MIN.*

I read it with one eye. I did not get up. This was, I want it noted, the most disciplined thing I'd ever done in a bed. The night before, I'd have been in the kitchen in my socks with the laptop open and a heart rate I could have charged a phone with. On the second night I'd done exactly that, and at 4 a.m. Maya had come out in her bathrobe, looked at me, looked at the screen, and gone back to bed without a word, which was worse than a word. There was still rice in the air vents of the car, which Maya called the confetti, and on the nightstand a single dried marigold petal that neither of us had been able to throw away.

The on-call phone belonged to Theo. It was his week. Ines, who usually held the nights, was in Lisbon until Friday, at her sister's wedding, and the runbook (twelve pages; *if the sync stalls, do this, then this*) said that when the first name was away, the second was on call. Under Theo's name on the schedule there was a third. It was mine. It had always been mine, in a gray font, the way a fire extinguisher has a name on it.

On the phone, a second line appeared. *ACKNOWLEDGED — THEO V.*

"You can look," said Maya, into the dark.

"I'm not looking."

"You're holding it over your face."

I put it down on my chest, screen in. The room was a cave. The radiator ticked. Beside me she turned onto her back, and I could tell, by the sound of her breathing, that she'd been awake for a while and had been deciding whether to say so.

"It's Theo's," I said. "He's got it."

"Does he know that you know he's got it?"

I opened my mouth. I closed it. It was a very good question, and I had no data.

At 3:19 the phone buzzed against my sternum. *Found it. Meridian pushed a store-hours update; Gilroy's on a different template. Fixing.* At 3:41: *Fixed. 187 enrollments caught up. No one missed a morning check-in.* At 3:42, a thumbs-up appeared under it, from me, before I'd decided to send one.

That was, more or less, the whole of my management philosophy that week. I texted *Thank you, great catch!* at 7:50. He wrote back, at 7:52:

*np.*

I looked at that for a good while, in the bathroom, with the toothbrush in my mouth. I knew what it was. Maya had taught me, over a year, to read a line like that. It was a very small word, with a period on it, and a period, in the dialect I'd learned at her table, meant *you missed it.*

*He's twenty-five,* I told the mirror. *He types like that.*

He did. He also typed *np* with no period, usually, when he was happy. I'd seen it a hundred times. I typed *You okay?* with my thumb, and looked at it, and deleted it, because it was 7:53 in the morning and he would say *yep.* I rinsed my mouth. I went to work.

---

Here is the first month, in numbers, because I was a person who still counted.

In the thirty-two days after launch, Meridian's forty stores asked a little over nine thousand people the question at the counter: *If you went quiet for a day, who would you want to know?* Of those, 3,912 said a name. More than half said *no, thank you,* and Carla's training said that was a complete answer, and a Meridian technician at Store 31 in Hollister, a man named Lucho, wrote on the store's feedback form that he'd never once, in nineteen years, been taught to hear a *no* as a success. He'd said, in the box marked *Comments,* that it was the first time the sign on the counter agreed with him.

In the first month, eleven enrolled people stopped answering for more than a day. Nine of them were reached by the person they'd named, inside the ninety minutes the system gives a named person, with a text, or a call, or in two cases a knock on a door. There were eight reasons. One was a phone in a lake. One was a man, seventy-one, who'd gone fishing and forgotten to say so. One was an accident, a fall in a bathroom, a woman's sister who got there at 8:40 with a key, and I'm not going to write anything more about that one because I don't have the right.

Two of the eleven were not reached inside the ninety minutes.

That is what I thought about, at night, in the weeks after. The report said *eleven of eleven resolved,* and it was true, and it had a green bar on it, and under it there were two lines in a smaller font that said *named person did not respond within the 90-minute window: 2.* They'd turned out to be fine. One was asleep. One had left the phone in a car. I'd looked at it for a long time at the glass table and moved on to the next slide, because there was a Thursday call with Carla in six minutes.

---

Carla, on the Thursdays, was a different woman than she'd been in the spring.

She was still dry. She still unfolded her hands when she was going to say something she'd written down. But she'd begun to say, in the first minute, *How are you?* and to wait, and I'd begun to answer, and we'd both pretended that was the agenda. The second Thursday, on the call, she said:

"It's the lines at the Saturday stores."

"Tell me."

"A Meridian technician has about ninety seconds with a person at the window on a Saturday morning." She'd had one of the store managers tally it. "The question takes thirty seconds. They ask it when they have time, and on a Saturday, they don't. The stores where it's quiet, nearly half the people say a name. The stores where it's loud, it's about one in ten." Her voice stayed level. "They're not refusing. They're skipping it. They don't want to hold up the line."

"That's a real problem," I said. I wrote it down. I wrote *Saturdays / the line / 30 sec* in my notebook, and underlined it, and said, "I hear you."

"I know you do."

There was a short silence on the line, as of someone folding something and putting it back in a drawer.

"I'll bring it to the team," I said.

"All right."

On the following Thursday I wrote it down again, in the same notebook, under the first one, and underlined that one too. I want it on the record that I listened. Remember that, later.

---

There was a pharmacy to run, too, and for the first time in a year it wasn't mine to worry about.

Kevin Chen started on the fourth of October.

He was the boy from City Hall, who'd come with his mother, Mrs. Chen, and a pair of glasses that he adjusted with the back of a knuckle. He was twenty-two. He'd finished a pharmacy technician certificate in the summer and been turned down, he told me later, by three chains, one of them Meridian, who'd said he lacked *customer-facing experience.* Maya had noticed that he'd stayed behind to help the clerk fold the programs, without being asked, and had gone to look for her own mother afterward and found him at the clerk's counter, wiping up a spilled coffee with his own handkerchief.

"You wanted a part-timer," said Suresh, over dinner. "You hired a person with a handkerchief."

"I wanted a person who'd notice," said Maya. She said it to her rice. "I'll teach the rest."

I'd been waiting for her to say something about it. I think it was the first time she'd made a decision about her father's pharmacy, her father's shelves and her father's computer, without anyone's permission, in front of her father, who looked at her for a moment across the dal. He nodded once. It was a nod I knew, from the other end of the same table, from the other father. It said *that'll hold.*

She began to come home at six.

That's an exaggeration; she came home at 6:20. The pharmacy closed at seven, and for ten years a Raman had locked the door, and now Kevin did it, on Mondays and Wednesdays, with a key on a green lanyard, the same kind I'd given Priya. She'd walk in, and put her bag on the hook (she'd taken off her coat at the pharmacy, which I understood by then to be the best indicator I had), and stand in the kitchen for about four minutes, doing nothing. I stopped asking what she was doing. She said, the third week, *This is when I come home,* and I understood that it was a thing that took four minutes, and that I'd been counting the minutes, and that what she needed from me was to stop counting.

---

It was on a Sunday in October that I had an idea.

It came exactly like the last one. I was at the kitchen table, with the laptop and the coffee, at the end of the month's report, looking at the *what's working* tab. There were four items on it. The first was *the question at the counter.* The fourth was *Carla's Thursday calls.*

And I thought about the thing I'd been doing, on Fridays, with Maya.

We had a Friday. It had started in the spring, with a walk, and then a dinner at the Thai place on Murphy Avenue, and then it had simply been Friday. Nobody had planned it. She'd said, one night, in the kitchen, *Fridays are ours,* and they were. It was the best thing in the week. It was clearly, I thought, the thing that was working, and it wasn't on the tab.

And it had a problem. It had a problem, which was that sometimes it didn't happen. In October it hadn't happened twice, once because of Meridian's training, once because a delivery ran late and she'd stayed to close. We'd missed it both times, and neither of us had said anything, and I noticed, at the kitchen table, the way I notice a dip in a column of data, that a thing that works but doesn't *always* work is an engineering problem.

I did not pause. I opened the calendar.

It was nine minutes of work. I made a recurring event. *Date Night — Maya + Nate.* Every Friday, seven o'clock. I gave it a location (*TBD*) and a reminder (thirty minutes before; ten minutes before; *Leave now?* at five) and, because the calendar offered it and I was in a fever of optimization, a video link, in case one of us was traveling. I invited her. I pressed *Send.* I leaned back in the chair.

It was, to be fair to myself, a lovely piece of work. It had a clean title. I felt something I hadn't felt since the morning I made the reminder, which I'd told myself I would never feel again, and I felt it in full, for about ten minutes, until I heard the front door.

---

She was back from her parents' dinner, standing in the kitchen with her phone in her hand and her coat half off.

"There's a link," said Maya.

"For when we're traveling."

"We're in the same apartment."

"It's a recurring event, it's—" I heard myself. I'd got as far as *recurring.* "It's for the Fridays."

"I know what it's for." She put the phone on the counter. It was lit up, and I could see the title from where I stood, in the same cheerful blue as every meeting I'd ever had, with a little camera on it. *Date Night.* Under it, *Leave now?* She read it aloud, slowly, to the room, in the voice she uses for a label with the wrong dose. "Date Night. Friday. Seven p.m. *Leave now?*"

"It's so we don't miss it."

"We missed it twice. We said so. We said it was okay." She hadn't raised her voice. She doesn't. She took her coat off the rest of the way, and she hung it on the hook. "It's in my calendar. With a link."

"Is that—" I said. "Is that bad?"

She looked at me for what felt like a long time. She had one hand flat on the counter. Her face didn't do any of the things I'd learned to watch for. She wasn't angry. She was thinking carefully about how to be kind.

"I like Fridays," she said. "I like that you come. When you come, it's because you came. I didn't *accept* anything. Nobody sent me a thing, and I didn't pick *yes.* You were just there, and so was I." She turned the phone face down. "If it has a link, I'm going to be thinking about the link."

"It's the Sunday reminder."

I hadn't known I was going to say it until I had. It sat there, on the kitchen floor, between us, like a dropped tool neither of us wanted to pick up.

"It's not the Sunday reminder," said Maya. "It's nicer. You didn't make me answer it." She almost smiled. "It's the same thing, though."

"I'll delete it."

"Don't make it a *thing.*" She picked up the phone. "Leave it. I'll know what it is." And then, because she is Maya, and because I'd done something for nine minutes in a fever that I'd have to wear for a while, she added: "I'm not saying don't make a calendar. I'm saying don't make it the reason."

I stood there with my hands in my pockets, in the kitchen, at 6:44 on a Sunday, in the light from the stove hood.

"Okay," I said.

She looked at me. She waited, a second, to hear which kind it was.

"That one's all right," she said.

I did the dishes. She dried. It's a strange thing to put in a book: that I deleted the series that night, while she was in the shower, and that on Friday I came home at 6:10 with a bag from the Thai place and no particular plan. She was on the couch, and she looked up, and said, *Oh,* the way you say it when a thing you'd stopped expecting arrives.

I'd gotten the lesson exactly right, about the calendar. I'd gotten it wrong about the other thing on that tab.

---

We hosted Thanksgiving, which is to say that Ray did, because he'd said, in October, in a phone call that lasted four minutes, *I've got the room. And the pond.* He said it as if the pond had been the deciding factor.

It was the twenty-fifth of November, and cold, and the light came across the lawn at about three in the afternoon, long and yellow, through the bare branches, and lay on the new water like a thing put down carefully. There were sixteen of us. This had been Lakshmi's number, not Ray's: she'd called him, the week before, and the two of them had had a conversation of perhaps ninety seconds, which I've since reconstructed as follows. She'd said, *What shall we bring?* He'd said, *Nothing.* She'd said, *What would you like us to bring?* He'd said, after a pause, *Is there any chance of a soup?*

There was a soup. There was also a turkey, which my father carved in the kitchen with his own knife, which he had brought, and which he'd sharpened in the car. There was a pot of rasam, and a dish of green beans done with coconut and mustard seed, and a tray of the stuffing that Ray's neighbor makes, and a second tray, of what Lakshmi called *the same thing, but with some opinions.* There was a pie. There were two pies. Arjun had flown in from Seattle on Tuesday and was asleep on the sofa under a coat by one o'clock, with one hand open on his chest, looking about nine years old, and Suresh, whom nobody had asked to, stood by the sofa and put a blanket over his son. Then he went into the kitchen and asked my father about the knife.

I'd have paid money to have a recording of what they said. I got only a piece. My father said: "It's a Wüsthof." And Suresh, with great seriousness, in a voice I'd heard him use on a bottle of insulin, said: "How often?"

"Every year. Before this."

"Ah," said Suresh. And they went on to a thing about a grinding wheel that I'm told lasted forty minutes.

We ate at four, at the table in the dining room, which was extended with a card table and a sheet of plywood under a cloth, and everybody was close enough to touch someone's elbow. Ray said grace. It was six words. They were: *Thank you. Denise would've liked this.* He sat down very quickly. Lakshmi, at the other end, who had not understood that this was grace, and had been about to say something, closed her mouth and, across the whole length of the table, set her hand flat on the cloth, toward him, not touching anything.

Priya wasn't there; she was at her mother-in-law's, in Sacramento, for the other half of the same holiday, which I'd learned is a thing people do. Kyle had come, with a pie he'd bought and an expression of purpose, and he'd been put next to one of Maya's cousins, a graduate student named Divya, who had met him at the wedding party and had translated about a dozen things said around him in Tamil, none of which, she told him, were about him. He'd asked her to repeat the last one twice.

At six, in the kitchen, there was a sink, and a window over it, and in the window the lawn had gone the dark blue of a screen with nothing on it. I stood there with a stack of plates. I'd been told, by the man who owned this house, that you don't do them alone.

"Who's drying?" said Maya.

She'd asked it at every sink since the summer before last, and I'd always thought it was a trick question. It wasn't. She took the towel from the oven handle, and stood next to me, shoulder to shoulder. I washed. She dried. Behind us, in the dining room, there were fourteen people at the end of a long meal, scraping the last of the pie.

"I have a question," I said.

"Mm."

"It's not a good one. It's whether I'm allowed to like this without making it a metric."

"Is it a metric?"

I looked at the plate in my hand. It was one of Denise's. There was a chip on the rim, at about four o'clock, that Ray had never fixed.

"No," I said. "I think it's a plate."

She laughed. She doesn't, always. It's a short laugh; it comes out of her nose, and then she puts her hand over it, as if she'd said something rude. "That's the first time," she said, "you've liked a quiet thing and not graded it."

My phone buzzed in my back pocket, once. I didn't take it out. I found out the next morning that it had been Theo, from his apartment in San Mateo, with the on-call phone and a bag of frozen dumplings, who'd taken the holiday weekend so that Ines could drive to her in-laws. He hadn't mentioned it. When I found out, I texted *thanks.* He wrote back *np.*

He'd have come to Ray's if I'd asked. I'd told him, in November, that he was welcome. Maya says those are different sentences, and that I would know the difference if somebody said one to me. I'd been standing at the sink, with my hands in a pan, when it should have occurred to me.

**FOUNDER'S NOTE**
*Scale what works.*

**TO:** All founders
**FROM:** A person who has read the memo
**RE:** Scaling the thing that works

Everything you were told is correct. When a thing works once, you should do it ten times. A good dinner is a recipe; a good meeting is a standing meeting; a good week is a template, and the template should have a link. If you've found a thing that works, put it on a calendar, give it an owner, and remove the part where it depends on someone feeling like it.

Please note the following exceptions, which apply to almost everything.

You may scale a process. You may not scale a person's wanting to be there. Those look identical for about three weeks, from the outside, on a dashboard, and then they stop looking identical, and the first one to notice is never the one who built the calendar.

Please also note that the thing that worked on Fridays was not Friday. It was that nobody had asked. The moment you ask, it becomes a different thing, and sometimes a better one. But it isn't the same thing. Do not report it as the same thing.

Some other findings from the first two months:

1. A customer who says *no, thank you* has given you the most useful sentence you'll hear all year.
2. A *great catch!* is not a thank-you.
3. When a good person, on a good week, types *np* with a period on it, the period is the message.

I wish I could tell you I knew the third one then. I knew it in the bathroom, with the toothbrush in my mouth; I filed it with the first two. The pattern, I've since learned, is that I always knew. It's what I did with it.

Regards,
N.
