# Sensitivity-read brief: *The Marriage Startup* (working title)

**What this is.** A contemporary novel (~90,000 words, first person) about Nate Calloway, a white Midwestern-born Silicon Valley founder, and Maya Raman, a Tamil-American pharmacist from Fremont, CA. They build a consent-first check-in service (Loopback) while falling in love and marrying. The Raman family runs a small independent pharmacy. We would like honest, specific reactions, not reassurance. Please mark anything that rings false, flattens, or would make you wince, and tell us what you would do instead.

**Format.** Page or chapter references are to the manuscript chapter numbers (these may shift in a coming restructure). Each reader gets only the relevant chapters.

---

## Reader A: Tamil / Tamil-American reader (family, language, ritual)

**Chapters:** 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23, 24 (all scenes with Maya's family).

**Characters.** Maya (Tamil-American, 30ish, guarded, dry); her mother Lakshmi (about 60, runs the counter, keeps a list of people she checks on); her father Suresh (about 63, pharmacist, formal, a fax machine, quiet); her brother Arjun (ER doctor in Seattle, appears Ch 19, 23); cousins (a graduate student, Divya).

**Questions**
1. **Register.** Suresh and Lakshmi speak in formal, contraction-free English ("I go to the UPS. You come." "I do not hire." "You will not speak."), while Maya, Arjun and cousins speak fluent casual American English. Does this read as a real Tamil-speaking immigrant couple in their 60s in the Bay Area, or as stage dialect? How would you fix it?
2. **Food.** Food is a love language throughout (the tins of idli, sambar and lemon rice; "Saapteengala?"; mysore pak as "a declaration"; rasam at Thanksgiving; aayasam). Is it authentic, overused, or a trope?
3. **Words and phrases.** Please check: *Saapteengala* (asking whether you've eaten), *Amma/Appa*, *aayasam* (used by Maya to name a feeling of being worn out "from being handled"; she says the closest she can get in English is "tired"), *nimmadhi* (used at the wedding party for a feeling of settledness, peace), *kozhukattai* (a steamed rice dumpling with a coconut-jaggery filling), mysore pak (a sweet), and a Tamil sentence rendered in English ("Nobody asks"; "We don't ask anyone for help, in this family"). Is the meaning, spelling, transliteration and usage right? Is the "no English word for it" device (aayasam, nimmadhi) condescending or fair?
4. **A family code.** The plot leans on the idea that Maya's family says "thank you" when they are angry, and "nothing urgent" or "no hurry" when something is urgent, and that they "don't ask for help." Does this feel like Tamil/South Asian family reticence, or a stereotype/generalization? Would you attribute it to Maya personally instead?
5. **The wedding (Ch 18–19).** A small family ceremony the day before City Hall: a priest from the temple, marigold sari, Lakshmi and Maya cook together, 21 people, Nate in a kurta?, then a civil ceremony at San Francisco City Hall with a clerk, then a party. Is the sequence, what the priest does, and what is said plausible? What details are missing or wrong? Would the family have a bigger ceremony, and how would Maya's choice to keep it small land?
6. **Intermarriage and friction.** The Ramans welcome Nate with very little friction. Is that credible? What friction would you expect from parents, aunts, cousins (about caste, community, language, religion, his job)? We plan to add a short scene of mild friction (an aunt asking whether he is Tamil; Arjun's initial skepticism) and would like your guidance.
7. **Maya's name.** She is Maya Raman; Americans keep mis-spelling it "Ramen," and she is quietly protective of it; we plan for her to keep her surname after marriage. Does that fit?
8. **Anything else.** Any scene where the Raman characters exist to validate or teach the white narrator rather than to live their own lives?

## Reader B: Domestic violence advocate (and a clinician if possible)

**Chapters:** 18 (the DNC free-text note "DV"), and the planned scene where Priya tells Nate about her cousin (to be written).

**Scene.** During a pilot, the system accidentally texts 31 patients flagged do-not-contact, one with a free-text note that says "DV." The team fears it reached an abuser's phone. It turns out the woman changed her number in April, so the text went to a stranger. The incident is meant to show why the product's consent rules matter. A planned follow-up scene has the cofounder Priya explain, in her own words, that a cousin changed her number and was found because of a text.

**Questions**
1. Is the incident plausible, and is the response (stopping all outbound messages, handing the 31 names to the pharmacy chain's own pharmacists to decide, no message to anyone else) sound? What would a real data-protection or DV-safety protocol do (e.g., safety planning, notification to the patient by a trusted channel, legal notification duties)?
2. Is the tone right, or does it use DV as a plot beat? What should be said, left out, or made less tidy?
3. The cousin story: what details of how someone is found through a number change are accurate, and what should we avoid writing (e.g., anything that works as instructions)?
4. Anything that would be unsafe, retraumatizing or misleading for a reader who has lived this?

## Reader C: Pharmacist and a cardiac/ER nurse

**Chapters:** 7, 8, 10, 15, 21 (a wrong-strength near miss; second check; "near-miss log" notebook), 22 (vaccination corner and consultation room), 23 (Suresh's chest pain; unstable angina; blood test "clear so far"; stent next morning; home Sunday; "no lifting for a week"; work part time), 6 and 8 (a man who fell, found by a neighbor/granddaughter).

**Questions**
1. **Pharmacy practice.** Is it plausible that Maya, as a pharmacist, catches a wrong-strength blood-pressure pill at a second check after the customer has left, runs into the parking lot, and swaps the bag? How would the store handle a near miss properly (reporting, documentation, notifying the patient, the "near-miss log" we describe as a spiral notebook by the register)? What is wrong or too casual? Is "tech turns down by chains for lack of customer-facing experience" plausible? A technician certificate?
2. **Retail details.** A single-pharmacist family pharmacy in a strip mall: hours, staffing, a hook on the wall for the staff coat, a laminated label sheet for a patient who can't read the label, a fax machine, lollipops in a jar.
3. **Cardiac and ER.** Is the scene with Suresh (gray at the table; ambulance; troponin negative at first; "unstable angina"; admission; cath/stent at 8:20 the next morning; discharge Sunday) realistic? What would you change? What would the family be told, and when?
4. **Discharge and follow-up.** The product's premise is that people go quiet after discharge. Does the nurse's account of day-three/day-ten home visits and a man alone for days ring true?
