# Publisher's editorial report (simulated): The Marriage Startup

Role-played acquisitions / editorial / marketing / sales team at a trade fiction house, 2026-10-01. Read all 24 chapters, premise, themes and the flags memo. Nothing was edited. Treat as opinionated advice, not instructions.

## 1. Acquisitions summary
- **Verdict:** Not as submitted. Yes on a revise-and-resubmit, conditional on: cut to ~85,000 words (shed ~14,000); break the one-flaw/one-lesson loop and add real stakes in Ch 9–14 and 18–22; give Maya her own career, flaws and plot; add the missing romance beats (no on-page kiss, no "I love you" between Nate and Maya); rebuild the Founder's Notes as a varied device; complete sensitivity reads (Tamil/South Asian, domestic violence, pharmacy/cardiology); open Ch 1 faster.
- **Shelf:** upmarket commercial fiction, "smart rom-com with a heart," book-club skew; closed-door, low heat. Secondary: workplace/startup fiction.
- **Comps:** The Rosie Project; The Soulmate Equation; Anxious People / A Man Called Ove; Remarkably Bright Creatures; Ayesha at Last. Tonal pitch: Ted Lasso for tech founders.
- **Readers:** women 28–55 in book clubs and upmarket rom-com; startup-adjacent professionals; engagement/wedding gift market; BookTok "wholesome" readers; children of immigrant families; nurses, pharmacists, clinicians.
- **Jacket pitch:** "If you went quiet for a day, who would you want to know?" Nate Calloway has never been asked it. He is a Silicon Valley founder whose first startup died quietly because he built it without asking anyone. At a mixer he'd rather not be at, he meets Maya Raman, who is waiting for 8:45 so she can leave. She has her mother's index card, a coat she won't take off, and a rule about the cliff after two coffees. What starts as customer discovery becomes Loopback, a check-in that notices when someone goes quiet. It also becomes a marriage, a pharmacy, a found family of stubborn people who keep lists, and an education in the difference between listening and showing up. Funny, tender and sharply observed, this is a love story about the unglamorous work of noticing.

## 2. What's working
1. The hook question and refrain ("Nobody's job is to notice").
2. Maya's first three chapters (wit, props, the coat, "an *effective* hour").
3. Consent as plot: "THE PERSON PRESSES THE BUTTON HERSELF," Maya's message rewrite, NO SECRET MODE, Ch 14 "Am I a project?"
4. Ray, Dan and "Text me when you get there" (Ch 4 → 11 → 19 → 22).
5. The Ramans' props: receipt roll, fax ("It says received. It does not say read"), "Two spoons. Not three."
6. Priya, Kyle, Jules ("That's not a slide," "Kind or useful?").
7. Set pieces: the clock night (Ch 10), the DNC/"DV" column and telling Carla within the hour (Ch 18), Carla's "You listened. Nothing changed." (Ch 21), the glass of water "I've got it" (Ch 24).

## 3. The big problems (ranked)
1. **A loop with no escalation.** ~18 of 24 chapters: Nate applies a system to a person, someone names it, shame, a plain sentence, "Okay" with no period. Spreadsheet/system errors in Ch 2, 5, 7, 13 (x3), 14, 17, 20, 21, 23. Stakes never bite: conflicts resolve within a chapter; Dr. Okafor does the business development; offers arrive in a chapter; the Series A closes off page; no competitors or cash crisis; Cole is softened. The DV scare (Ch 18) resolves by luck. *Fix: make one thing cost something.*
2. **Structure and pacing.** Chapters are strikingly uniform (3,093–4,576 words) until Ch 18–19 (5,531, 6,180). The middle (Ch 9–15) is a plateau. The Ch 19 wedding climax is ~78% in; Ch 20–24 (~20,700 words, 21% of the book) hold a second climax (Harold Beck / Carla) and several low-tension episodic chapters. Ch 18 holds four plots; Ch 19 three payoffs plus cameos.
3. **Nate: likable and funny, but passive and over-explained.** The climactic action in more than half the book is Nate staying quiet; he is examined by a succession of wise women. He narrates his own growth ("the hardest thing I did that year") and foreshadows ("Remember that, later"). Measured tics: "I'd like to say/the record" ~75; "the way you'd/like a man" similes ~100; "Hm" 100+ across 8 speakers; "a thing" 89; "very" 63; flat-hand gestures ~50; coat 72; "Okay" beats ~90; recycled images ("put the smile away like a receipt," "adding a number to a column," "the plain gray type of a thing built in a hurry").
4. **Maya: a person for seven chapters, an instrument after.** She supplies the product's key decisions and fluent therapeutic self-diagnosis; no work scenes after Ch 5; her career is never dramatized; "My name on my door" has no plant; she is almost never wrong. (Partially addressed: Ch 9, 21, 23 now have Nate asking for what he needs.)
5. **Romance beats missing.** No on-page kiss (only "kissed me on the cheek," Ch 13); no "I love you" between Nate and Maya (the only on-page "I love you" is Maya to her mother, Ch 18); winston: "Take your time." *(Verified by search.)*
6. **Secondary cast.** Priya is the true second lead (promote); Kyle stops being funny and turns sentimental; Ray's tells repeat; Cole/Margo are types; Theo underwritten; everyone speaks in finished aphorisms; a chorus of sages.
7. **Product and startup plot.** Charming, well-glossed, but thin on business model, HIPAA/security, sales cycle; product drift (SOS, microphone, wall device vanish); rescues are summarized, never dramatized live.
8. **Emotional beats and humor.** Beats are earned but templated (quiet moment, hand on counter, silence, head on shoulder); the narrator labels significance; humor is front-loaded and becomes callbacks after Ch 14.
9. **Cultural representation.** Ramans are specific and competent, but: formal, contraction-free speech reads as stage Indian English; food-as-love is a trope; "I don't have it in English" (*aayasam*, *nimmadhi*) exoticizes; "thank you means furious" generalizes; little interiority or friction for Maya as a daughter marrying out; helpers mostly validate Nate; DV as a plot-data beat. *Sensitivity reads:* Tamil/Tamil-American; DV advocate; pharmacist and cardiac nurse; reader from an immigrant pharmacy family.
10. **The ending (Ch 24).** The napkin payoff and "I've got it" land. Problems: "My name on my door" unplanted; the pregnancy is telegraphed (six listed clues); the Note repeats the body nearly verbatim; the child address is unplanted; pregnancy-as-final-beat is a genre cliché; don't let the sequel hook flatten the finality.

## 5. The Founder's Notes (as the editor saw them, before the Ch 9/21/23/24 edits)
Marketing gold (screenshot-ready; a 24-week pre-pub serial). Craft: placed last, so they restate; one lesson in new clothes (ask first, listen, don't fix, say it early); readers will skim by Ch 10; later ones preach in imperatives. Recommended: fewer, front-loaded (~12–14, full form in Ch 1–6, then irregular); change the form (Ch 8 the folded napkin unreproduced; Ch 13 the reminder itself; Ch 14 ~50 words; Ch 16 the card; Ch 18 a stopwatch log; Ch 21 the post-mortem timeline); let them be wrong; epigraph some; collect as a one-page "Nate's Playbook" in back matter; Ch 24 a ~90-word letter to the child.

## 6. Market and packaging
- **Titles:** *Text Me When You Get There* (strongest; needs clearance); *Nobody's Job to Notice*; *Ask Me One*; *Is Anyone There?*; *The Second Person*. Avoid "lessons"/"business" subtitles.
- **Cover:** illustrated, warm, two-color; a coat on a hook, an index card, a gray text bubble *You okay?*
- **Opening pages:** Kyle's speech runs ~560 words and Maya appears ~word 1,900; the "investors are the hot girls" metaphor is dated and sour; open at the ballroom door or the cup and backfill Kyle in <150 words.
- **BookTok/book club:** "Which okay is it?"; "Text me when you get there"; "Who would you want to know?"; the coffee count; the "Ask me one" game; the toolbox card. Discussion themes: noticing vs. caring; when help becomes handling; family codes; notification vs. person; what you inherit from silences.
- **Content notes:** grief, referenced domestic violence, medical emergencies, estrangement, pregnancy. Closed door.
- **Series:** moderate; the Priya spin-off is strongest. **Launch:** pub Jan–Mar 2028; trade paperback original with French flaps; run the Notes as a serial from the autumn before.

## 7. Revision plan (12 changes)
1. Make something cost (Ch 9–14, 18). 2. Break the loop: one spreadsheet mistake per act. 3. Give Maya a plot and flaws (licensed pharmacist who left retail; plant "my name on my door"; a work scene; a scene where she's wrong). 4. Add the missing romance (first kiss Ch 6; "I love you" at a specific moment; wedding kiss; a date with banter). 5. Fix the back third (split Ch 18; merge Ch 20 into 21; compress Ch 22; denouement ~13,000 words). 6. Rebuild the Notes. 7. Purge the tics (budgets: "I'd like to say" ≤8; "Hm" ≤25; "the way you'd" ≤15; flat hand ≤12; "quarter turn" ≤4; "very" ≤20; "a thing" ≤30; coat halved). 8. Open Ch 1 faster. 9. Make the product legible (150-word explanation; a live catch; a competitor; pricing; a security review; "we never built the microphone"). 10. Cultural authenticity pass. 11. Fix the narrative frame (plant the child address or cut it). 12. Rebalance length (84,000–88,000 words, target ~86,000; cut ~14,700, add back ~3,000).

## 8. Closing note from the editor-in-chief
You have a real book: a brilliant hook question, a first act with genuine wit, set pieces that made this room go quiet, and an ending that earns its tears. What it lacks is a story that keeps getting harder. Readers will put it down because the chapters feel the same by Ch 10; the stakes never threaten anything they love; the romance never gets to kiss, laugh or fight at full volume; and the Founder's Notes, which should be the hook, have turned into a recap they can skip. Fix those, give Maya a life that runs without Nate, make Priya a co-lead, and let one thing actually cost someone something.
