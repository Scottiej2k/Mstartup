# Founder's Note rewrite samples, v3 (not applied to the chapters)

Direction from Scott: practical notes to himself, in Nate's voice. "In business it's like this, which helped in my relationship like this." Concrete enough that a reader could use the lesson. Sometimes the parallel works; sometimes he applies a business rule to Maya and learns he can't treat the people she cares about like resources on a project. One direction only (startup to relationship). The "you" (his unborn child) appears only in Ch 24.

---

## Ch 1: *Ask what's actually wrong.*

In business: the first answer a customer gives is the one they give every vendor. I heard *inventory* from every pharmacist for eight months and built an inventory tool nobody used. A quick test: if the answer would be the same no matter who asked, it's the vendor answer. The real one comes after you ask for the last time it happened, and then say nothing. Maya's came on the second try: Mr. Peralta, and her mother's receipt roll.

With Maya: she says *fine,* *busy,* and, eventually, *nothing urgent.* Same test. If I'd get the same sentence from anyone, I haven't been told anything yet. So I stopped asking *how was your day* and started asking *what's the part you'd skip?* Then I wait. Ten seconds feels like a minute. I count.

It doesn't work if I'm asking for the answer I need. I learned that with customers first. They can tell.

## Ch 6: *Ship the ugly version. Ship it that night.*

In business: put the first version in front of one real person this week, while it's embarrassing. Loopback's had two boxes and a button, and it broke in front of the one doctor we needed. She signed up anyway. The first version isn't the product. Its job is to get you a second version, which is whatever the person's reaction tells you to change.

With Maya: I missed Jules's birthday because the demo broke, and then spent a whole Sunday writing the perfect apology. Six drafts, none sent. Everything I knew about shipping said to send the bad one, so I walked up four flights with the wrong wine and said what I had. It was a good version one, and it only worked because it came the next day.

Version two is the part people skip. Mine was a cake, baked from a video in Kyle's mother's pan, for the friend I'd stood up. Jules called it structurally honest. Say sorry badly and quickly. Then change one visible thing.

## Ch 7: *A list of people is not a list of leads.*

In business: when someone already has the trust of a hundred and forty people, you've found the hardest thing to build, and the sensible thing is to ask how to reach them. A good founder counts that as an asset. I've kept those spreadsheets.

With Maya: I stood in her family's stockroom and looked at her mother's receipt roll and said the word *network.* She said, "Those are people. Not leads." I'd applied a rule that works on a market to a woman's mother, her father's store, and the names she'd grown up reading off a wall. It wasn't unkind. That was the problem.

The rule I've kept: when I catch myself doing business math on something Maya loves, I stop and ask what it is to her before I ask what it's worth. Quick check: would I say this sentence out loud to her mother? If not, I don't say it to Maya's face, either.

---

## Ch 9: *Optional is not free.*

In business: don't commit until you have to. Cole's phrase was *stay liquid,* and for a company with fourteen thousand dollars in the bank, that's sound. You keep options open to get information. The rule has a built-in test: if you're still gathering information, wait.

With Maya: I applied it to a dinner table. A woman asked who Maya was, and I said *friend,* because I'd told myself I was respecting her rule about names. I wasn't researching anything. I knew my answer. When more information can't change the decision, keeping the option open stops being prudence and starts being a door with a draft. It cost Maya three days of one-word texts. She'd been holding one open, too, on her phone.

The question I ask now before I hedge: *what would I learn if I waited another week?* If the answer is nothing, I'm not being careful. I'm standing in someone's hallway.

## Ch 11: *Say the unwritten part out loud.*

In business: a founders' agreement is for questions nobody wants to ask while they still like each other. Who owns what. What happens if either of us leaves. What happens if we take money. Priya made me answer all three on paper, in April, and added a line of her own: neither of us can take investment without the other. We had the argument over paper, while we liked each other, which is the cheapest time to have it.

With Maya: I tried to carry the company's tools in the door. A vesting cliff is fair between business partners: trust earned in installments. I made a joke at my parents' table about a one-year cliff in a marriage, and nobody laughed. Maya wasn't afraid I'd leave in a year. She was afraid there was a schedule.

What transferred was the habit, not the form. Say the hard terms early and out loud, before they have to be argued. And leave out the instruments, because she isn't a partner I'm vesting. On a back step I said, *There's no schedule. You're in.* That was the whole agreement.

## Ch 21: *If you've written it down twice, change something.*

In business: a blameless post-mortem has four steps. Write the times down the left. Write what each person knew. Write what made it hard. Never write who. Then make one change, no bigger than the problem. We used it on outages, and I'd never thought of using it at home.

With Maya: I used it at the kitchen table, at ten at night, after a message that said *nothing urgent* sat unread for six hours. Times down the left. It found no villain. It found a code, two decent people using it to leave each other alone. The change was small: when she tells me something, I ask, *do you want to tell me, or do you want it fixed?*

The other rule came from Carla. I'd written her Saturday-line complaint in a notebook twice and underlined it, and I thought that was listening. Now the rule is: if I've written the same complaint down twice, the third time I owe a change, not a note. Maya's was a stool at the pharmacy, Wednesdays at four.
