## Original options (for reference)

## Option A: Clinical scheduling / care-coordination software
- **Pitch:** Software that helps small clinics and pharmacies coordinate patient
  follow-ups.
- **Why it works:** Ties into Maya's world (her family's pharmacy, her work).
  Gives her real expertise and real skepticism. Forces the narrator to learn
  from customers the way he must learn from her.
- **Risk:** Healthcare has heavy regulation. Can be used as texture rather than
  a subject.

## Option B: Developer infrastructure tool
- **Pitch:** A tool that engineers love and executives buy.
- **Why it works:** Classic Valley story; easy to depict; lots of comedy.
- **Risk:** Abstract and disconnected from Maya's world. Fewer places for the
  product to comment on the relationship.

## Option C: A consumer app about... communication
- **Pitch:** Something to help people stay connected.
- **Why it works:** Direct irony (a founder who builds communication tools and
  can't communicate).
- **Risk:** Too on-the-nose unless played with a light touch.

**Recommendation:** Option A. But this is your call.

## Company beats (product-agnostic)

2. **Founding team:** Narrator + co-founder.
3. **Prototype:** Ugly, works for one user.
4. **Early users:** Small wins, first real feedback.
5. **First paying customers.**
6. **Seed round:** Pitching, term sheets, dilution, obligations.
7. **Team build:** Hiring, culture, first departure.
8. **Growth:** Metrics rise, intimacy falls.

   The company nearly dies.
10. **Recovery/pivot.**
11. **Series A / stability.**
12. **Next horizon:** New product, new risks (the sequel hook).

## Vision: Loopback is a "pulse check" (APPROVED, Scott 2026-09-29)
Loopback isn't only about pharmacy refills. The underlying idea is general:
**noticing when someone has gone quiet, and making sure a person follows up.** There
are many reasons someone might need a pulse check:
- a patient who stops picking up their medication (the healthcare wedge);
- someone who lives alone;
- new parents;
- an elderly relative far away;
- anyone whose silence would otherwise go unnoticed for too long.

**Healthcare is the first vertical, on purpose:** it's where a missed check-in costs the
most, the pain is clear, and Maya's pharmacy gave Nate the first real customer
insight. But Nate has ambition to expand into more verticals, and investors love the
bigger story. The tension: a wide vision pulling at a company that still has to make
one thing work for one kind of customer.

How it shows up in the book:
- **Ch 3:** interviews hint at the general idea (someone mentions a tenant who lives alone), and Nate deliberately stays on healthcare.
- **Ch 10:** VCs want the "huge market" story; Ray: "Nobody wants a market. Somebody wants a Tuesday to go right."
- **Ch 21:** the pivot to a named person with protected time works in any vertical.
- **Ch 24:** the "new product" hook is the pulse-check idea widening into a second vertical (e.g., new parents or people who live alone).

## Killer feature: the opt-in second person (added from Scott's comment)

When a patient signs up, they can opt in to naming one secondary person (a daughter, a neighbor, a friend) the pharmacy is allowed to contact. If the patient goes quiet after the pharmacy's check-in text, the pharmacy follows up with that person the next day to make sure the patient is okay. This is the part of the pulse check that makes sure a person follows up. It is opt-in only. The patient-facing text ends with reply codes: "Reply A to acknowledge this message or U to unsubscribe." Texts never name the medication or condition.
